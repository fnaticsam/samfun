Fear and the Shadow

At a certain depth the work stops being pleasant. The self-structure, feeling its grip loosen, produces a fear that seems to have no object. This is not a sign that something has gone wrong. It is the sign that inquiry has reached the root.

In plain words

Fear is contracted life energy. When the little self relaxes its grip, the same energy that was fear becomes aliveness; nothing is added or removed. The path does not go around the dark parts of the psyche; it goes through them, and the hero's task in every mythology is not to slay the dragon but to ride it.

Primordial fear

Practitioners who reach this point describe the same thing: a fear that is not of anything. Not fear of a snake or a deadline, but fear itself, a feeling of not being in control, of imminent dissolution. The mind rushes to supply objects: I will go mad, I will faint, I will lose my family, I will end up homeless, I will die. None of these is the fear. They are stories the mind wraps around a sensation it cannot explain.

What is actually happening is that the "I"-thought is giving way. Teachers who have taken students through this consider it good news: it means the work is correct and has reached the depths. Nobody gets an easy ride at the root of the self-structure. When it gets real, it gets real. And the moment fear of this kind arises is the single best moment to inquire: to whom does this fear belong? Who is watching this character go through its letting-go?

It helps to reassure the character. What feels like death is not death. The conditioned self is moving from the driver's seat to the back seat. It will still be part of the team; it will still get to play. Over time it discovers that what comes through when it stops steering is better than anything it could do alone: less predictable, more alive. The phrase "die before you die" is ancient and accurate, but it frightens the character, so it is worth translating: let go of control before your body dies, and see what is left.

The roller coaster

Consider what happens on a large roller coaster. Climbing the first hill, you can feel fear building in the body. There are two ways to ride. In the first, at the top, something flips: you become the witness, the emptiness riding the coaster, watching the drama from a remove. It is peaceful, and it is not really riding. In the second, something gives way entirely: every sense opens, the energy opens, the emptiness is still there but so is all of the fullness, and the ride is life itself riding, connected to the whole park. Emptiness riding is a real stage. Non-dual riding is where it is going: form and emptiness together, fear and aliveness revealed as one energy.

If you hold on, the energy contracts and there is only survival. If you let go, the identical energy is bliss. It is the same with cold water: plunge in tense and it is shock; let go into it and, in time, the nervous system learns that the shock was the resistance. Practitioners who have been through the primordial fear many times report that the same energetic movement that once terrified them now simply passes through as bliss. The body learns that letting go does not mean oblivion.

Hope and fear are one thing

Fear cannot be dropped on its own, because it is half of a pair. Hope is craving projected into the future (may this happen); fear is aversion projected into the future (may this not happen). They are the fourth skandha, wearing two masks. The "I"-thought is strongest exactly here, in hoping and fearing, and this is not a flaw in the design; it is the pulse of expansion and contraction that runs through everything, the in-breath and out-breath, yin and yang, Shiva and Shakti.

Over the gate of hell in Dante's Inferno are the words "abandon all hope, you who enter here". Read as instruction rather than threat, it is exact. Hell is the ego structure. To get through it you abandon hope, and you abandon fear, because they are the same mechanism. What is left when both are gone is not despair. It is presence. Seen clearly, fear is like the Wizard of Oz: a terrifying facade with nothing behind the curtain. Or the classic snake in the rope: frightening until you see it is a rope, and then there is nothing to be frightened of, and there never was.

The cave

Joseph Campbell, who spent his life mapping the hero's journey across cultures, summarised its turning point in one sentence: where we had thought to find an abomination, we shall find a god; where we had thought to slay another, we shall slay ourselves; where we had thought to travel outward, we shall come to the centre of our own existence.

The clearest modern telling is in the first Star Wars trilogy. Luke, training with Yoda, is sent into a dark cave. Yoda tells him he will not need his weapon. He takes it anyway, meets Darth Vader, strikes him down, and the mask falls open to show Luke's own face. Every real journey has this scene. The thing you feared most in the dark turns out to be a disowned part of you.

Carl Jung called these disowned parts the shadow, and he was unambiguous about the direction of the work: "One does not become enlightened by imagining figures of light, but by making the darkness conscious." And: the hero's main feat is to overcome the monster of darkness, "the long hoped-for triumph of consciousness over the unconscious". Much of modern spirituality is about imagining figures of light. It does not work, because whatever is left unconscious continues to run, and we call the results fate, or karma. Make it conscious and it is simply part of you.

Ride the dragon

Our stories have regressed. The old myths knew the dragon must be integrated; the new ones mostly have it slain, light conquering dark, good conquering evil. Look at the oldest images of the Buddha, or of Patanjali, in the ancient temples: they sit surrounded by nagas, serpents, sometimes seven of them fanning out behind the awakened one. The serpents are not being fought. They are the dimensions of the unconscious, integrated, at attention, part of the whole. Modern iconography dropped them, and something was lost.

The serpent has been demonised for two thousand years: the spiral, the feminine, the manifested world, the power of becoming. Wars have been fought and people burned out of fear of it. But that power is simply nature, life itself, and only the ego ever goes to war with it. The story of the fall makes sense read this way: Lucifer, the most beautiful angel, splits off, "rather to rule in hell than serve in heaven". That is the ego, exactly: my will, not Thine, as if they were two. The fallen angel is the angel that became an ego. The demon and the angel are one energy; reject the demon and you have rejected the angel too.

This is why the shadow returns in relationships, synchronicities and triggers. Whatever you push away in yourself shows up in the world as a mirror. The person who repels you is showing you an exile. Integration does not mean acting the shadow out, and it does not mean befriending everyone who carries it. It means noticing what in yourself is being resisted, feeling it fully, and letting it come back into the one.

Two horses

The Bhagavad Gita pictures the person as a chariot: the horses are the senses and desires, the reins the mind, the charioteer the discerning intelligence, and the passenger the Self. Plato's Phaedrus has a sharper version with two horses. The black horse, eros, pulls down toward the material, toward sensation and pleasure and the world. The white horse, thumos, pulls upward toward the spiritual, toward truth, toward what is higher. The charioteer must drive both.

The teaching is in what Plato does not say. He does not say kill the black horse. Kill it and you have lost half your power; the chariot cannot move. Let the white horse run unchecked and you neglect the body and the world, and the chariot goes off the road just as surely. The horses are the skandhas, and energy follows consciousness: make them conscious, harness both, and the chariot goes where the current is going. The material and the spiritual are not enemies. They are the team.

Go deeperThe energetics of fear

Not everyone feels this, and it is not required, but for those who do the pattern is consistent. The habitual centre of "I" sits around the brow, the third eye. As practice deepens, energy moves up from there toward the crown, and the sense of "I" has to give way for that movement to complete. If the little "I" is clinging, that is the moment fear arrives. With repetition, the same movement becomes a small, blissful event rather than a terror. Kundalini teachings describe this as the awakening of evolutionary potential through the body's centres, the chakras; the crown is where the personal current rejoins the whole.

Some traditions use literal darkness. A darkness retreat, several days without sun or moon, removes the circadian control mechanisms of duality. After three or four days something changes; the body may release its own visionary chemistry, and there is no longer dark and light but, in the words of those who have done it, only luminous emptiness, a waking dream that goes on forever. It is the cave, taken literally.

Jung also spoke of the collective unconscious. In deep clearing, what surfaces sometimes does not feel personal: generational, archetypal, like a primordial scream from something larger. Birth experiences, death experiences, huge reconfigurations of energy. The honest position is not to know what it is, and to trust that there is an intelligence to it. Grounding helps: a barefoot walk in nature, ordinary tasks, sleep.

Sufism: "Die before you die" is a saying the Sufis attribute to the Prophet, and fana, the annihilation of the self, is their name for the passage. Rumi: "Don't turn away. Keep your gaze on the bandaged place. That's where the light enters you."

Christianity: St John of the Cross's dark night of the soul is the classic map of this stage, a passage of fear and loss that the soul cannot skip and that ends in union. His own verse: to come to what you know not, you must go by a way you know not. The descent into hell before resurrection, and Dante's road through the Inferno to Paradise, encode the same route.

Buddhism: on the night of his awakening the Buddha was assailed by Mara, the lord of illusion, with armies of fear and daughters of desire. He did not fight; he touched the earth and remained. Tibetan iconography is full of wrathful deities, terrifying forms that are enlightened energy wearing the face of what the ego fears.

Hinduism: Kali, black, garlanded with skulls, dancing on Shiva, is the destroyer and the mother at once. Her devotees do not slay her; they surrender to her. Shiva himself is destroyer and lord of yoga, the serpent around his neck.

Taoism: the yin, dark, receptive, downward, is never the enemy of the yang. The Tao Te Ching praises the valley, the female, the low, the dark: "know the white, keep to the black".

Practice: tracking fear in the body

Whenever fear, dread or a sense of losing control arises, in sitting or in life. Also once a week as a shadow exercise, step 5.

  1. The moment fear appears, leave the story. Whatever the mind is saying will happen, set it down for now. Do not argue with it; just do not follow it.
  2. Find the fear in the body. There is always a change: a contraction in the chest or belly, heat, a shift in the breath, tingling in the hands. If you "don't feel things in the body", look for the subtlest change in the breath; it is always there. This is the second skandha, raw sensation, before the label "fear".
  3. Stay with the sensation and track its whole pattern as it moves. Let it be exactly as strong as it is. You are not making it go away; you are making it conscious.
  4. When there is a little space, inquire: to whom does this fear belong? Who is aware of this body going through this? Rest as that for as long as you can.
  5. Shadow, weekly: bring to mind a person or quality you find repellent. Ask honestly: where is that in me, and what have I exiled to keep it out? Feel whatever the question stirs. Do not fix anything; just let the exile be seen.

If the fear escalates into overwhelm with no equanimity at all (sweating, shaking, a feeling that the system is going down), stop and ground: walk outside, feel your feet, drink water, sleep. Return when stable. A frightened nervous system cannot integrate anything.

Self-check

What is fear, energetically?
Contracted life energy. When the self-structure relaxes its grip, the same energy is experienced as aliveness and bliss. Nothing is added or taken away; only the resistance changes.
Why can't fear be dropped without dropping hope?
They are the same mechanism, craving and aversion projected into the future. Dante's gate says abandon all hope; the ego structure is hell, and both hope and fear must go to pass through it.
What does the hero do with the dragon?
Rides it. Integrates it. The ancient images show the Buddha surrounded by serpents at attention, not slain. What is pushed away in oneself returns as the world; what is made conscious becomes part of the whole.
Why not kill the black horse?
Desire and the material pull are half the chariot's power. Kill them and it cannot move; let the white horse run alone and it leaves the road. The charioteer harnesses both in balance.
Keep
  • Primordial fear means the inquiry has reached the root. Inquire there: whose fear?
  • Fear and aliveness are one energy; the difference is holding on or letting go.
  • Hope and fear come into being together. Abandon both to pass through the ego's hell.
  • Enlightenment is made by making the darkness conscious, not by imagining light.
  • Slay nothing. Integrate the serpents; drive both horses.
