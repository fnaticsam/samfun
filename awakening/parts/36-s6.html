Stage 6 of 10

Absorption

As sitting deepens, the mind passes through recognisable stages of absorption, and some of them are the most beautiful experiences a human being can have. That is precisely the danger. This stage maps the territory and names the one mistake almost everyone makes in it.

In plain words

The Pali word jhana (Sanskrit dhyana, from which come Chan and Zen) traces back to a root meaning "to burn up". Meditation burns up conditioning, and as it burns, energy is freed and consciousness changes, layer by layer. These states are real, they are signs of progress, and none of them is who you are. Mistaking a state for your true nature is the one mistake that keeps the whole game of "I got it, I lost it" going.

What burns, and in what order

Here is the general shape of the progression, held loosely. It is far better to discover these things in your own sitting than to hunt for them with a checklist.

The hindrances. At the start, the mind is a whirlpool of preference: craving, aversion, restlessness, doubt. This is dukkha, unsatisfactoriness, as a felt state. Practice here is mostly the work of Stage 4, cultivating enough equanimity that the hindrances stop being hindrances and become the way in.

Energy breaks through. When reaction to preference slows, something gives. Energy the Pali texts call piti (rapture, or simply freed prana) rises, and the felt tone shifts from struggle to ease: from dukkha to sukha, satisfactoriness. This is the first jhana, and it feels good. It can arrive as tingling, warmth, waves, light, geometric colour, sound, or a change in the state of consciousness itself.

Letting go of the good feeling. A new game begins immediately: the mind learns that this can be produced and starts preferring it. So the next thing to release is sukha itself. Anyone who plays the sensation game long enough discovers it is endless; states arise and pass, and chasing them is samsara with a nicer decor. The insight of impermanence, anicca, begins here as a felt fact rather than a doctrine. Letting the states come and go is a deeper equanimity, and another layer of mind drops.

Single-pointedness. The small waves settle. The mind becomes one-pointed, energy builds, and there is a stage, often the fourth jhana, where the part of the mind that distinguishes pain from pleasure goes quiet. People report tireless energy, as if they could sit forever. This is the end of the "material" jhanas, the ones still connected to body and sensation.

Mind and body drop away. Keep sitting and the body-mind falls off. The "immaterial" jhanas are rarely discussed because there is little point: consciousness experienced as infinite; then states the mind cannot even frame, such as "neither perception nor non-perception". Very subtle layers of self drop here. Dogen's definition of meditation, "to drop off body and mind", refers to this.

Hold all of this lightly. If it becomes a set of boxes to tick ("got the first one, second one covered"), it has become dharma as another possession of the ego. A menu describes the meal; nobody is nourished by eating the menu. The map is useful only so that when the territory appears you are not frightened, and not fooled.

Samadhi with a seed

The deepest of the technique-produced states is the union experience: being one with everything. In the yoga sutras it is savikalpa or sabija samadhi, samadhi "with a seed", because a seed of the self-structure survives inside it and will sprout again. In Buddhist terms it is the fruit of the material jhanas. It is temporary, as every state is. And it is the peak experience of a human life.

Which is why it should come with a warning. Consider a common story. A beginner at a meditation retreat, who still fully believes they are the character, has this experience. They walk out under the stars and are the stars; the tree is them; everything is them. The character thinks: this is it, this is what the Buddha and Christ had, I did it. Then, as every state does, it fades. And in the fading, the seeker is born: a desperate hunger to get it back, which drives years of sutras, techniques, teachers, retreats, and a growing pile of knowledge that only moves them further from it. The seeker finally fails, completely, in some place with no escape, having tried every trick. The mind is humbled; it realises it knows nothing about its true nature. And in that failure, with nothing left for the character to do, the conditions are right for the flip.

The lesson is not that union is bad. It is that it is far better to awaken to what you are before having the union experience, because the experience, arriving to someone who still believes they are the character, almost guarantees the creation of a seeker.

Two samadhis

Savikalpa samadhi is the peak of the experience side of practice. A technique — breath, body scan, chant, concentration on energy — carries attention into union with its object. The breath is no longer over there; everything is you. It can be the most beautiful experience of a life, and it is temporary because it is still an experience.

One practitioner met it as a beginner during a body-scan retreat. Walking afterwards, the trees and stars were not separate from him. He thought the character had become enlightened. The state vanished in sleep, and the next morning its absence felt like a fall from the highest place to the lowest. A seeker was born and spent a decade collecting teachings and techniques to get it back. The more the character tried, the further away it seemed. Only complete failure revealed who had been struggling.

Nirvikalpa samadhi points the other way. Mind and body drop off altogether. There is no world, no sense object, no phenomenon — identical to deep sleep except that primordial awareness is awake. Nothing appears, yet there is unmistakable continuity. From this comes the unshakeable knowledge that what you are does not die; birth and death belong to form. A deep kenshō is this recognition: not merely that everything is you, but that you are none of what appears.

Kenshō may first be clear only while awake. “Full kenshō” names continuity through waking, dreaming and deep sleep. The Sanskrit map calls a taste of this turīya, the fourth that underlies the other three states. Turīyātīta, or sahaja samādhi, is its permanent, natural form. Life remains fully available: work happens, conversation happens, and there is a sense of watching the character live without being confined to it.

The distance between a glimpse and a great master is not a more impressive experience but relentlessness. Ramana Maharshi awakened and would not give the recognition up. For most people it brightens in deep practice and dims in ordinary life. The invitation is not to imitate his outer life, but to value continuity more than any state passing through it.

The one mistake

There is really only one mistake on this path, and it perpetuates everything else: mistaking a state, any state, for the truth of who you are. The state is phenomena. It is in time; it comes and goes; the mind can remember it, or thinks it can. What it actually remembers is the energy, the feelings, the light, the sense of expansion. It cannot remember the awareness that was present, because awareness is not an experience. It is what the experience appeared in, and it is here now, unchanged, while you read this sentence.

So when the mind says "I had it and I lost it", ask what "it" was. If it was the beautiful phenomena, then yes: gone, as all phenomena go. But the one who knew the phenomena was not part of the phenomena. The awareness in the peak experience is the awareness here now. Nothing about it has changed; only the show around it changed. Recognise that, and there is nothing to get back.

Picture the whole of human experience as a line. At one end, total union, love, bliss. At the other, total identification, separation, hell. Every moment of a life is somewhere on that line. Who experiences the entire line? That one is what these stages are trying to reveal, and it is not at either end. When identity flips from the character travelling the line to the awareness the line appears in, there is a peace that is not contingent on where the character currently is. It is not the joy at the happy end of the line. It is something of a different order: the relief of knowing what you are.

Polishing a tile

A Zen student sat with great diligence, following every rule and working hard to become enlightened. One day his teacher sat down beside him with a flat stone tile and began rubbing it. The student tried to keep meditating, but at last asked what the teacher was doing. “Polishing this tile to make a mirror.” The student stared. A stone can be polished all day and will never become a mirror. The teacher’s reply was the lesson: that is what you are doing with the conditioned self.

The self-structure is the tile. It can become calmer, kinder, better informed and exquisitely spiritual, but no amount of improvement turns it into awareness. When practice becomes a project by which “I” will awaken, the tile is polishing itself. The effort may prepare the ground, yet the one claiming the effort cannot cross the last step.

The teacher keeps polishing until the student's project is exposed as impossible.

So let the mud settle. Do not stir the water in order to make it clear. Sit without adding another task, another judgement or another picture of success. This is just being: not passivity, and not a special state produced by the mind. As the whirlpool slows, recognise who is aware of the stillness. The mirror was never manufactured by the tile. It is what has known the polishing, the confusion and the quiet from the beginning.

Go deeperYoga and Buddhism: the same ladder, two languages

Patanjali divides samadhi into stages that map onto the Buddhist jhanas with roughly ninety percent agreement, which is remarkable for two systems that developed separately. The first four jhanas, the material absorptions, correspond to samadhi with seed. Beyond them, Patanjali names nirvikalpa or nirbija samadhi: seedless, without support, in which the mind-body structure drops off entirely and no seed remains to regrow. The yogic word for the last of his eight limbs, samadhi, is the goal of the seventh, dhyana, which is the same word as jhana and Zen.

Go deeperUnion in the other traditions, and its aftermath
SufismChristianityHinduismTaoism

Sufism describes the union state as fana, annihilation of the self in God, and is equally clear that it is not the end: baqa, subsistence, is the return to ordinary life with the self gone and God remaining. Al-Hallaj's "I am the Truth" was spoken from fana; the tradition remembers both his ecstasy and the price of announcing it.

Christian mysticism has a large literature of "consolations", the sweetnesses that visit the contemplative, and an equally large literature warning against attachment to them. St John of the Cross's dark night is, in part, the withdrawal of consolations so that the soul stops loving the gifts and learns to love the giver. Teresa of Ávila's interior castle moves through mansions of increasing absorption toward a union that is not a passing rapture but a settled marriage.

Hinduism: the Gita distinguishes the yogi who merely tastes samadhi from the one established in it (sthitaprajna, of steady wisdom), whose peace is not disturbed by the arrival or departure of any state. Ramana Maharshi called the temporary absorptions kevala and the permanent, open-eyed state sahaja, the natural state.

Taoism is suspicious of peak states on principle: the sage is ordinary, "like an uncarved block". Where the other traditions warn against clinging to the summit, Taoism quietly declines to climb it.

Practice: who knows this state?

Whenever a notable state arises in sitting, pleasant or unpleasant. Also once, deliberately, with a memory of the best moment you have ever had in meditation.

  1. Let the state be fully present. Do not dampen it and do not chase it. Have the complete experience of it; without grasping, you experience it more, not less.
  2. Notice the mind's first move: a flicker of "yes, keep this" or "no, make this stop". That flicker is the fourth skandha, the seed. Feel it and let it be.
  3. Ask, without straining for an answer: who is the knower of this state? Not what the state is, but what it is appearing to.
  4. Notice that whatever answer arises is itself another state, another thing known. Let it go too. Rest as the knowing.
  5. When the state fades, and it will, notice whether the knowing faded with it. Check honestly. That which did not fade is what this whole path is about.

The mind will complain that it cannot find the knower. Correct: the knower is not findable as an object. The mind's failure here is not your failure. It is the beginning of the mind learning its limits.

Self-check

What does the word jhana literally mean, and why does it matter?
"To burn up." Meditation burns up the samskaras, the conditioning. Each stage of absorption is a layer of the conditioned mind being consumed, freeing energy and changing consciousness.
What is samadhi "with a seed"?
The union experience produced through technique: being one with everything. A seed of the self-structure survives inside it, so it is temporary and the self returns. Beautiful, valuable, and not who you are.
Why can't the mind remember samadhi?
It remembers the phenomena: the energy, the feelings, the light. Awareness is not an experience and leaves no memory trace; it is here now, unchanged. "I had it and lost it" is always about the phenomena.
Why is it better to awaken to true nature before the union experience?
Because union arriving to someone who still believes they are the character almost guarantees a seeker: the ego claims the experience, loses it, and spends years trying to get it back.
Keep
  • The jhanas burn conditioning in layers: hindrances, then struggle, then the preference for pleasure, then body and mind.
  • Every state is a signpost, and every state passes. Do not eat the menu.
  • The one mistake: taking a state for your true nature. This is what breeds the seeker.
  • The awareness in the peak experience is the awareness here now. There is nothing to get back.
