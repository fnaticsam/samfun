Stage 4 of 10

Sitting

There are hundreds of meditation techniques and they all do the same two things. Once you see what those two things are, the technique matters much less, and what comes up while you do it matters much more.

In plain words

Every technique combines concentration (staying with one thing) and surrender (not reacting to what arises). Together they interrupt the habit pattern of the mind. When the pattern is interrupted, its defences come up as hindrances: pain, sleepiness, restlessness, doubt, a busy mind. These are not obstacles. They are the exact material that needs to be made conscious, arriving on schedule.

What every technique has in common

Breath-watching, body scanning, mantra, gazing, chanting: strip any of them down and you find a focusing element and a letting-go element. Concentration and equanimity. Attention and surrender. When these two are present together, the brain's neuroplasticity is at its highest; neurons that fire together wire together, and what is being wired is the capacity to be present without reacting.

The path is therefore a paradox, and it is better to know that at the start than to discover it as a disappointment. From one side there is no path: the awareness you want to awaken to is already here, and there are no steps to arrive at the present moment. From the other side there clearly is a path: identification is progressively loosened, and things happen in a recognisable order. There is a path until there isn't. Techniques are how the conditioned self interrupts its own pattern, creating conditions in which the flip is more likely. When the flip comes, you see that it was always the character doing something it thought it needed to do.

An old image: a technique is a thorn used to remove a thorn. Once the first thorn is out, you throw both away. Hold onto the technique, keep repeating it, believe it is special, and it becomes another layer of the self-structure, another control mechanism. Ramana Maharshi's warning is blunt: to let the mind run the practice is to make the thief the chief of police.

Three words that are not the same word

Concentration is the camera. It can be turned toward the breath, a sound, a point between the eyebrows. It can be narrow (air at the nostrils) or wide (the whole body expanding and contracting). Turning toward and setting the width: that is concentration.

Attention is the continuity of staying with the object through time. Attention span. Patanjali's image is oil poured from a jar: an unbroken stream, not drops. Concentration chooses the object; attention keeps it.

Awareness is neither. It is what the camera and its continuity appear in. It has no direction, no width and no quality; it is not something you turn or hold. Krishnamurti called it choiceless for exactly this reason: the mind chooses where to point, and awareness is simply aware of that choosing. When these pages say "rest as awareness", they never mean "point your awareness somewhere". They mean stop pointing.

The eye of the bird

In the Mahabharata, the archery master Drona sets a wooden bird in a tree and has his students take aim at its eye. As each draws the bow he asks: what do you see? One sees the bird, the tree, the other students, his own arrow. Dismissed. Another sees, in his mind's eye, the arrow entering the eye. Closer, but no. One cleverly says there is no bird. Nice try. Last comes Arjuna, and when asked what he sees he says: I see only the eye of the bird.

That is single-pointedness, and it is the key to the whole progressive path. When you take aim at your meditation object, there is nothing else. Not looking around, not reviewing how it is going, not deciding whether this is the right technique. Only the breath. The same instruction appears in St John of the Cross's ascent of Mount Carmel: climb straight up, looking neither left nor right, not stopping to pick the flowers and not frightened off by the creatures on the path. And it appears in Taoism as the reason the method works: chi follows consciousness, so when consciousness is gathered into one point, energy gathers with it, and the burning-off of conditioning begins in earnest.

It does not much matter what the object is. Breath, energy in the body, the space between the eyebrows, a chant: each is a bridge into the same field of aliveness the yogis call prana. Watch the breath long enough and you become the breath; chant long enough and you become the sound. The watcher and the watched merge. Breath is the bridge, energy is the bridge, and every road leads to the same water.

Hindrances are the material

Apply single-pointed attention and surrender for more than a few minutes and the mind's defences arrive. Tiredness, a sudden need to move, an itch, doubt about the whole enterprise, a mind running scenarios like a television left on. The classic error is to treat these as things going wrong. They are the self-structure showing you exactly where it is contracted and unconscious. A hindrance is an invitation to bring consciousness to some part of your being. Even the question "how do I overcome sleepiness?" contains the mistake, because the little self that wants to overcome it is part of the sleepiness.

There are two ways to meet any hindrance, and both arrive at the same place.

Inhabit the pattern

Take sleepiness. It has a shape: heaviness around the eyes, a particular feeling in the head, a change in the breath, a slumping or swaying in the posture. Bring the entire pattern into view and inhabit it. How does it change over time? Is it continuous or does it come in waves? What does the breath do when it arrives? A hindrance can only take you over while it runs in the shadows. Fully seen, it becomes intimate, then it loses its power, and then it can drop. This is the phenomenological approach, and the body scan (below) is its most developed form.

Inquire directly

Alternatively, when tiredness arises, ask: to whom does this tiredness belong? Who is tired? Clearly the mind-body is tired. But am I the mind-body? There is an awareness that is aware of the tired body; who is that? If the inquiry lands, there is an instant flip: people who were exhausted a moment before light up, because the tiredness is still there and it has nothing to do with them. This is the direct approach, and Stage 7 is devoted to it.

Which to use is a matter of intuition and of where you are on the path. The self-structure is a candle; it does not matter which end you burn.

Two techniques worth learning

Labelling thoughts

For the mind that will not stop. Every thought is either an image (visual), an inner voice (auditory), or both. Sit with your anchor, and each time a thought pulls you away, simply note it: "visual", "auditory", or "both". Do not enter the story; do not argue with it; do not evaluate it. Note the type and return. Something surprising happens: even a heavy, frightening thought, once noted as "auditory", pops like a balloon. Repeated for a session, the technique reveals recurring patterns and drains their energy. You are using the mind to break a habit of the mind.

The body scan

For samskaras held in the body. Move attention slowly and systematically through the body, part by part, head to feet and back, observing whatever sensation is present in each part with equanimity: no preference for pleasant over unpleasant, no lingering, no skipping. Where there is nothing to feel, note that too and move on. The scan brings attention to the second skandha, raw sensation, exactly where the unconscious knots are stored, and equanimity is what lets them release. It is the same principle as inhabiting a hindrance, applied to the whole body.

Productive and unproductive pain

The invitation to sit without reacting has a limit, and it is important to know it. Productive pain is karmic: aches that only exist while sitting, the "squirm", restlessness, discomfort that is the pattern of resistance itself, held in the body and rising to be felt. The way out of it is through. Unproductive pain is the intelligence of the body reporting a real problem: an old injury, a nerve, a warning sign such as light-headedness, dry throat and vision closing in. That is not to be pushed through. Adjust the posture, use a cushion, switch to walking meditation, stop. Never brutalise the vessel.

The same distinction holds psychologically. There is a middle way between blasting through and babying yourself: move beyond the comfort zone, but not so far that the nervous system goes into overwhelm. Sweating, shaking, a sense of losing control that has no equanimity in it at all: these are signs to step back, ground the body (a barefoot walk outside works), and return when stable. Someone once asked a monk what he was doing in Las Vegas; he said anyone can be a monk on a mountain top. A noisy apartment or a difficult life is training with weights on. But weights can be too heavy, and only you can feel where that line is.

Go deeperDirect path and progressive path

Teachers tend to fall into one of two camps. The direct-path teachers point relentlessly at the awareness that is here now, with no on-ramp: you either get it or you do not. Krishnamurti dismissed techniques altogether because it was so obvious to him that awareness is choiceless and already present. The progressive-path teachers give a method and hold that doing it faithfully leads to liberation; the Vipassana tradition of the body scan is a pure example. Both are right, and both arrive at the same samadhi.

The Buddha taught at every level, which is why his teaching has both. The famous Flower Sermon was pure direct pointing: he held up a white flower in silence, and one student in the whole assembly understood. Everyone else needed intermediary steps. Ramana Maharshi's image was of dry kindling that ignites at once versus wet wood that needs time to dry. Most people are wet wood for a while, and then are not. A reasonable approach is to work with a technique until concentration and presence are established, and then let the technique fall away into simply sitting, simply being.

The yogic term for the last three limbs of Patanjali's eightfold system, practised together, is samyama: dharana (concentration, the effortful part), dhyana (absorption, the surrendering part) and samadhi (where doing and non-doing merge). Every technique is a version of the first two, aimed at the third.

Go deeperPractical questions that always come up

Lying down. Generally discouraged, because almost everyone falls asleep, and because sitting is what allows energy to build and break through. There is one rule in most Zen halls and it is no lying down. The exception is someone who reliably stays awake lying down and is integrating a strong energetic release; then lying can let the energy move.

Moving. If the body wants to move, ask who is moving. Sometimes it is the conditioned mind seeking relief and comfort; sometimes it is energy beginning to move the body on its own (see kriyas in the field guide). Learn to tell them apart. Dissipating the build-up through movement brings relief but never the breakthrough; there is an alchemy that happens only when the little self reaches the point where something has to give.

Noise. Explore dropping the concept of the sound ("annoying dog") and feeling the raw vibration as it arrives. If it is truly overwhelming the nervous system, use sound or ambient noise; otherwise treat it as weights on the equanimity muscle.

Controlling the breath. A little settling breathwork at the start is fine, but notice when the controller has taken over. Let the body be breathed. What happens when the one managing the breath relaxes?

Stiff hips, restless legs, a back that will not sit. Two years of ordinary stretching yoga or qigong has made the difference for many people who could not sit long enough for energy to rise. This is not spiritual; it is plumbing.

Practice: a twenty-minute sit

Daily. Same time, same place if you can. A timer, a straight spine, eyes closed or lowered. Do not move for the full twenty minutes unless real pain requires it.

  1. Aim. Choose one object for the whole sit: the breath at the nostrils, the rise and fall of the belly, or the point between the eyebrows. Only the eye of the bird.
  2. Stay. Keep attention on the object like oil pouring from a jar. When it breaks, notice the break without comment and return. A hundred returns is a hundred repetitions of the skill, not a hundred failures.
  3. Label. When a thought pulls you off, note it silently as "visual", "auditory" or "both", then return. Do not enter the story.
  4. Inhabit. When a hindrance arrives (sleepiness, pain, restlessness), turn toward it for a while. Find its full pattern in the body and breath. Then either return to the object, or ask to whom it belongs.
  5. Scan. For the last five minutes, sweep attention slowly from the crown to the feet, part by part, meeting whatever sensation is there with no preference. Then rest with no object at all for the final minute: nothing to aim at, nothing to do.

If light-headedness, dry throat, ringing or tunnel vision come on, stop: that is the body's intelligence, not a samskara. If you finish and the mind says "that was a bad sit", remember that the murkiest sessions are often the ones that do the most.

Self-check

What two elements does every meditation technique share?
Concentration (single-pointed attention on an object, sustained through time) and surrender (equanimity toward whatever arises). Together they interrupt the mind's habit pattern.
How is "awareness" different from concentration and attention?
Concentration is the camera turning and focusing; attention is keeping it there through time. Awareness is what both appear in. It has no direction, no width and no quality, so it cannot be pointed; it is choiceless.
Sleepiness arrives in your sit. Name the two valid responses.
Inhabit it: bring its whole pattern (eyes, head, breath, posture) into view until it is fully conscious. Or inquire: to whom does this tiredness belong, and who is aware of the tired body? Both lead to the same place.
When should you stop rather than sit through discomfort?
When it is unproductive pain: the body's intelligence signalling a real problem (injury, faintness, tunnel vision) or the nervous system going into overwhelm with no equanimity left. Karmic pain is sat through; the vessel is never brutalised.
Keep
  • Concentration plus surrender is the core of every method; the object is a bridge, not the point.
  • See only the eye of the bird. Chi follows consciousness, and single-pointedness is what starts the burning.
  • A hindrance is not a hindrance. It is the unconscious showing you where to look.
  • Techniques are a thorn to remove a thorn. Use them, then put them down.
  • Know the difference between karmic pain and the body asking for help.
