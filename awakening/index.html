Stage 1 of 10

The Current

Awakening is not something you achieve after a long, hard journey. It is a direction that life is already moving in, like a river that will reach the sea whether or not anyone pushes it. The work is to stop pushing against it.

In plain words

There is a character called "you", with a name, a history and a set of opinions. And there is the awareness in which that character appears, the way a drama appears on a screen. Suffering happens when the one watching forgets it is watching and believes it is the character. Awakening is remembering. Nothing about the show has to change.

A river, not a mountain

Most spiritual language makes awakening sound like a summit: rare, distant, reached by a few after heroic effort. It is more accurate, and far more useful, to picture a current. Everything that comes out of the source is moving back toward it. In the old cosmologies this was drawn as a torus, a continuous loop of going out and returning, involution and evolution, with no part of it standing still. Awakening is simply the point in that loop where the return becomes conscious.

This matters practically. If awakening is a summit, then you are at the bottom and the whole thing depends on your effort. If it is a current, then the most useful thing you can do is stop swimming upstream. The traditions call this effortless effort: it takes everything you have, and none of it is directed at making something happen.

Certain conditions make the shift more likely, and that is what the rest of this path is about. But it is worth knowing from the start that people wake up in every kind of circumstance. Whenever the habitual pattern of "me" is interrupted, whether by a death, a loss, an illness, or an unexpected joy, a gap opens. What the wisdom teachings provide is a map of the mechanism, so that when the gap opens you recognise it, and so that you can remain awake instead of falling back into the dream.

The character and the one watching

Sit in front of a gripping television drama for long enough and something curious happens: you tense when the protagonist is threatened, your heart races in the chase, you feel wronged when they are wronged. For a while, you have forgotten that you are a person on a sofa. No one would say you were bound by the drama. You were simply absorbed in it, and the absorption ends the moment you remember where you are.

This is the whole situation in miniature. The character on the screen is the conditioned self: the body, the personality, the memories, the running commentary. The one on the sofa is awareness. It is always the character that suffers. Awareness has never suffered, never been trapped, and never been set free, because it was never caught in the first place. Which produces the paradox at the centre of every awakening teaching: you were never bound, so you can never be liberated. You can only discover that the binding was a mistake of identity.

The kingdom of God is not coming with signs to be observed. Nor will they say, "Look, here it is!" or "There!" For behold, the kingdom of God is within you.

Luke 17:20–21

The Christian mystics read this passage the way the Zen masters read their own: the kingdom is already here, and the only reason it goes unseen is that we look through the false self, the little "I" that is always somewhere else. Nothing needs to arrive. Something needs to stop filtering.

Why bother?

It is fair to ask whether all of this is worth the effort, especially since people who glimpse it very often lose it again. Some have the experience, then the ego claims it ("I did it, I awakened") and builds a spiritual persona out of the memory, which can be a more stubborn ego than the original. That is common enough to be treated as part of the path rather than a failure of it.

The honest measure of benefit is simple: when you look back over your life, is there less suffering? Practitioners who have been at this for decades report the same thing. Looking back twenty-five years, there was exponentially more suffering and identification, and now it is clear who was suffering. It was always the character. The benefit of awakening is freedom from that delusion: a life that is not scripted, not run on autopilot by patterns laid down in childhood, and a peace that does not depend on how the show is going.

There is a second benefit that is easy to overlook. Beyond freedom from the character there is freedom to: to grow, to explore what this human vessel can become when its energy is no longer spent maintaining a fiction. The traditions describe this as the growth of the inner lotus, the maturing of the human instrument. Awakening is the beginning of that, not the end.

Choosing without a chooser

A question always arises here: if I am not the character, who makes decisions? Do I just drift? The answer that comes from direct experience is that in the awake state there is no chooser and nothing chosen, and yet things very much happen. Life keeps moving, decisions keep occurring, but they arise the way a spider builds its web: with ease and precision and no one standing behind the process approving each strand.

The dualistic, planning mind was never designed to steer a life. It is an interface, superb for arithmetic, language and navigating the physical world, and it has been promoted into a job it cannot do. When the interface becomes the dictator, the result is the constant low-grade friction most people call normal. When it returns to its proper role, the traditions speak of choiceless awareness: feeling the current of life and moving with it. The character can still choose to fight that current, and that is exactly when suffering returns, because the current always wins.

The martial arts carry the same teaching in the body. The lineages that trace back to Bodhidharma at Shaolin (the same teacher whose sitting practice became Zen) train the fighter to drop the thinker. If you are thinking about the next move, you are already finished. The energy moves from the belly, the heart and the brow in a single dance, and the "doer" is nowhere in it.

Go deeperAlways being Buddha, always becoming Buddha

Two words are used loosely and it helps to separate them. Awakening is the immediate recognition of unconditioned being, available in any moment, because the awareness being recognised is already here. Liberation is the long stabilisation that follows: the conditioned self learning its place, its momentum running down, the human vessel maturing until awareness can shine through it without interruption. The Zen phrase captures both at once: always being Buddha (nothing to attain), always becoming Buddha (endless unfolding).

This is why sadhana, spiritual practice, changes shape at the point of awakening. Before it, practice looks like effort: techniques, overcoming hindrances, a mind in motion trying to still itself. After it, the illusion of the doer is exposed and practice becomes simply abiding as awareness. Ramana Maharshi, asked about his own practice, said that he had never done any sadhana. He was speaking as the Self, which requires no cultivation. His body sat in a temple for years; that was the character being placed in conditions of no escape while the self-structure cleared. The two statements are not in conflict.

A rule of thumb from the same teaching: the conditioned mind makes a good servant and a poor master. Practice is not about killing the servant. It is about ending its tenure as master.

Taoism gives the image its clearest form: the Tao is the way things flow, and the sage practises wu wei, non-forcing, moving with the current rather than against it. "Heaven and earth have no preference." Water, the Tao Te Ching says, benefits everything and competes with nothing, because it is content to seek the low places.

Hinduism (Vedanta) says the individual self, Atman, is not other than the ground of everything, Brahman. The Bhagavad Gita's counsel to Arjuna to act without attachment to results is the same instruction as choiceless awareness: let the action happen, and stop claiming it.

Buddhism speaks of Buddha-nature, the awake quality already present in every being, obscured rather than absent. Dogen: practice and enlightenment are one, because you are not manufacturing awakening, you are expressing it.

Sufism describes the return to the source as the whole purpose of the soul, and the dropping of the separate self as fana, annihilation in the Beloved, followed by baqa, abiding in what remains. Rumi's verse is full of rivers running back to the sea.

Christianity holds the same current under the words "not my will but Thine", and in the mystical reading of "the kingdom of God is within you". The false self stands between the person and a kingdom that is already present.

Practice: the one on the sofa

Three minutes. Anywhere. Best done the first time somewhere quiet, and afterwards in the middle of ordinary life.

  1. Notice whatever is most vivid right now: a sound, a sensation in the body, a worry, a plan. Let it be exactly as it is.
  2. Now notice that you are aware of it. Not the thing, but the awareness the thing is appearing in. Do not try to locate that awareness or give it a shape. Simply notice that it is here.
  3. Let a thought come, one with some charge to it, and watch it the way you would watch a scene in a drama. It plays; it passes. Notice that the watching did not come and go with it.
  4. Ask lightly, without hunting for an answer: which of these is me, the scene or the watching? Then let the question go and rest as the watching for the remaining time.

If the mind says "I can't find awareness," that is fine. The mind cannot find it, because the mind is one of the things appearing in it. Noticing that you are aware is enough. Nothing is supposed to happen.

Self-check

Who suffers, the character or awareness?
Always the character. Awareness has never suffered, which is why the teachings say you were never bound and can never be liberated. The only thing that changes is the case of mistaken identity.
What does "effortless effort" mean, if it is not laziness?
It takes everything you have, but none of it is aimed at producing awakening. It is the effort of continually not swimming upstream: not resisting what is arising, not reaching for what is not here.
If there is no chooser, how does anything get done?
Things happen the way a spider builds a web, with precision and without a supervisor. The planning mind still works, as an interface. What drops away is the sense that a separate "I" must approve each move, which was never doing the work anyway.

Stage 2 of 10

How a Self Is Built

The sense of being a separate "I" is not a thing you have. It is a process that runs, moment by moment, in five steps that happen so fast they look like one. Watch them in slow motion and the whole structure becomes visible.

In plain words

A mosquito lands on your arm. Before you have swatted it, five distinct operations have run: a body, a raw sensation, a label ("mosquito"), a reaction ("bad, get rid of it"), and a faculty of attention that watched the whole thing. The Buddha called these the five skandhas, the aggregates. Together they manufacture the feeling of "me". None of them, on inspection, is a self.

The mosquito, in slow motion

Imagine you are sitting in meditation and a mosquito begins to whine near your ear. Slow the next two seconds down and this is what runs.

1. Form (rupa)

The baseline: there is a body here, sitting in space. Something physical for contact to happen on. The vibration of a mosquito landing on skin.

2. Sensation (vedana)

Raw sensory input, prior to any label. A tickle, a buzz, a change in the field of the skin. At this layer it is neither good nor bad; it is simply energy registering. This layer is important because it can be felt directly, and most of us have not felt it in years. We skip straight to the next step.

3. Perception (sanna)

The mind opens its library of memory and finds a match: "mosquito". Language, concepts, recognition. Still no preference has formed. This is the part of the mind that names things, and it is very fast.

4. Habit patterns (sankhara / samskara)

Now the charge arrives. Past experience with mosquitoes (itching, malaria warnings, ruined nights) fuels a reaction: "bad, must be eliminated". This is the layer of craving and aversion, of "I want" and "I don't want". It is where suffering enters, and where the sense of "me" is thickest, because the preference feels like mine. The Zen poem you will meet in Stage 5 calls this labelling of things as good or bad "the disease of the mind".

5. Consciousness (vijnana)

The faculty of attention that observed all of the above. It behaves like a camera: it can point here or there, zoom in tight or take in a wide view. It feels like the most "me" of all the layers, the witness inside. But look closely and even this camera is a function, not a person. It has no owner. It, too, is empty of self.

What makes this model valuable is not its elegance but that every one of the five can be caught in the act. These are not philosophical categories; they are discrete operations you can watch in your own mind this afternoon. Once they are seen, they lose their automatic grip, because a process can only run you while it runs unnoticed.

The cave and the classroom

Plato described prisoners chained in a cave, facing a wall, watching shadows cast by a fire behind them. Because the shadows are all they have ever seen, the shadows are reality. Anyone who is released and walks out into the sunlight comes back with news the prisoners cannot use.

The shadows are the contents of the mind: thoughts, images, perceptions, the running commentary. We are trained toward them from the crib, where a mobile is hung above us so that we learn to fix on objects. In school, a child at a desk notices a squirrel in the yard; there is aliveness in that squirrel, and attention goes to it naturally. The teacher, focused on the lesson, says pay attention. The child learns quickly that following the natural flow of energy gets you into trouble, and that pleasing the teacher does not. The false self begins here: a cultivated network of "shoulds", inherited notions of right and wrong, a persona built to keep out of trouble. Nobody did anything wrong. It is simply how the game starts.

Awakening, in this image, is coming out of the cave. Those who have described their first glimpse often use exactly these words: it was like walking out into a world I did not know was there. And, like the freed prisoner, there is no real way back once you have seen it, though you can certainly try.

Energy follows attention

Put your attention on your left hand right now. Within a few seconds there is tingling, warmth, a sense of aliveness that was not noticeable a moment ago. Nothing changed in the hand. Attention went there, and energy followed.

The Taoists put this as a formula: chi follows consciousness, and consciousness follows chi. Modern teachers say where attention goes, energy flows. It is the single most practical principle on this path, because it explains how the self-structure is powered. The five skandhas only have power because attention is given to them. The camera of the mind swings toward a preference, energy pours into it, the pattern strengthens, and the pattern pulls the camera back next time. That loop, running unobserved for decades, is what a personality is.

It also explains why the way out is not a fight. When attention stops feeding the loop, whether by resting as awareness or by fully seeing the pattern, the energy trapped in it is released. The body fills with it. The practitioners of every tradition describe this release in the same terms: as if there were no longer a body, only an ocean of aliveness. The Sanskrit compound for it is sat-chit-ananda: being, consciousness, bliss. The bliss is uncaused; it is not produced by any of the five skandhas. It is what is left when they stop consuming the energy.

Go deeperThe witness is still inside the dream

Many practitioners arrive at the fifth skandha and stop. They discover a detached watcher behind thought and take it to be their true nature. It is a real discovery and a genuine stage, but the traditions are unanimous that it is not the end. The witness and the witnessed arise together; they are the two poles of a single duality, the yin and yang of the mind. As long as there is a watcher here and a world there, you are still inside maya, the play of appearances.

The first four skandhas can be summarised as "the field of change" and the fifth as "the observer of change". Meditation and self-inquiry both aim at realising the entire mechanism, all five, as empty of self. When that is seen, the fifth collapses into the field it was watching. The Heart Sutra's line, "form is exactly emptiness, emptiness exactly form", is the report of that collapse. Stage 8 describes what riding a roller coaster feels like from the witness, and then from beyond the witness.

This is also why it is said that awareness has no quality. The camera has qualities: direction, focus, width. Awareness is what the camera and its images appear in. You cannot point it anywhere, because it is not a thing that points.

Vedanta describes the person as five sheaths (koshas) wrapped around the Self: the physical body, the energy body, the mental body, the body of discernment, and the body of bliss. Like the skandhas, the sheaths are examined one by one and found to be coverings rather than the thing covered. The Kena Upanishad asks what it is that the eye cannot see but by which the eye sees, and answers: that alone is Brahman, not what people worship as an object.

Taoism works the energetic layer directly. Its vocabulary of chi, the three dantian (the energy centres of belly, heart and brow) and the circulation of energy through the body is the practical science of vedana, the second skandha, and of the principle that chi follows consciousness.

Christian mysticism speaks of the old self and the new, or, in Thomas Merton's phrase, the false self and the true self. The false self is exactly this cultivated persona: "the self that is made of shoulds". Paul's distinction between living "according to the flesh" and "according to the Spirit" is read by the mystics as the same distinction between the conditioned mechanism and what is prior to it.

Sufism names the conditioned self the nafs, and describes its purification through stages, from the nafs that commands toward its cravings, through the nafs that reproaches itself, to the nafs at peace. The mosquito's fourth step is the commanding nafs at work.

Practice: five steps in slow motion

Ten minutes, seated. Repeat with any small irritation during the day: a sound, an itch, a text message.

  1. Sit quietly and wait for something to arrive: a sound, an itch, a twinge, a passing thought. Do not go looking; let it come.
  2. Find the raw sensation first, before the name. Where in the body is it? What is its actual texture, before you call it anything? Stay here longer than feels natural. This is vedana, and it is the layer we usually skip.
  3. Notice the label arriving: "itch", "car", "thought about work". Notice that the label is a separate event from the sensation, and later than it.
  4. Notice the charge: the flicker of wanting it to stop, or wanting more, or the story that starts to spin. Do not act on it and do not suppress it. Just see that it is a fourth event, distinct from the other three.
  5. Finally notice the attention that has been tracking all of this, swinging toward it like a camera. Ask lightly: is this camera me? Or is it also something being watched?

The point is not to stop reacting. It is to see the sequence. A sequence that has been seen is no longer automatic, and energy stops draining into it on its own.

Self-check

At which of the five steps does suffering enter?
The fourth: habit patterns, where the perception is labelled good or bad and craving or aversion fires. Sensation and perception are neutral. This is why the practices work at the level of preference rather than trying to stop sensing or naming.
Is the witnessing consciousness (the fifth skandha) your true self?
No. It is a faculty, like a camera, with direction and focus. It arises together with what it witnesses, so it is still inside duality. Awareness is what the camera and its images appear in, and it has no direction because it is not a thing that points.
Why does the self-structure lose power when it is simply observed?
Because it runs on attention. Energy follows consciousness; the skandhas only have power because attention feeds them. Seeing the sequence clearly withdraws that automatic feed, and the trapped energy is released.
Keep

  • Raw sensation comes before the label and before the reaction. Learning to feel it is the whole first half of the path.
  • Energy follows attention. The self-structure is powered by being looked at.
  • The inner witness is real, and it is still part of the mechanism.

Stage 3 of 10

Momentum

If awakening is a recognition, why does anyone need to practise? Because recognition does not stop a pattern that has been spinning for forty years. This stage is about karma, understood not as cosmic bookkeeping but as inertia.

In plain words

Awakening is like pulling the plug on a ceiling fan. The blades keep turning for a while on their own momentum. That momentum is your accumulated conditioning. Every time you react to a preference, you plug the fan back in. Practice is the art of not plugging it back in, for long enough that the blades come to rest.

Samskaras: grooves in the riverbed

Every strongly charged experience that was not fully felt at the time leaves a residue. The Sanskrit word is samskara (Pali: sankhara), often translated as "impression" or "formation". Picture water running over soft ground: it cuts a channel, and the next rain runs down the same channel, deepening it. Samskaras are the channels. They are where energy will flow unless something diverts it, and they are stored in the body as much as in the mind, as knots of contraction around the chest, the belly, the throat.

This gives a clean definition of karma that does not require any belief: karma is the tendency of energy to follow established pathways. Nothing more mysterious than a riverbed. When consciousness slips back into an old pattern, energy flows down the old channel and the pattern reinforces itself. When it does not, the channel begins, slowly, to fill in.

The fan

In the moment of awakening, identification with the mind and its reactions ceases. The plug is out. But the mind-body is a physical system with momentum; it does not stop because the power has been cut. Thoughts keep arising, preferences keep firing, the personality keeps performing. The blades are still going round.

Here is the crucial point, and it is easy to miss. As long as the plug stays out, the fan slows and eventually stops. Every time you identify with a preference the mind produces and react to it, you have reached over and plugged the fan back in. Not because you are bad at this, but because that reach is the habit. The entire discipline of the path after awakening comes down to noticing the hand reaching for the socket.

Stated in physics: a mind in motion stays in motion unless acted on by an outside force. All spiritual practice is that outside force, a consciousness that diverts energy from its conditioned pathways. This is why someone can meditate for years and change nothing if they go straight back to the same routines, relationships and defences. The wiring stays in place because it is being used. You have to be willing to change your life, not as a moral requirement but because the old life is the socket.

The two knots

Of all the strands that tie the sense of "I" to the character, two are primary. The body is attached to comfort. It seeks pleasant sensation and avoids discomfort without pause, which is the mechanism of an animal ensuring its survival, now running in a creature that is not in danger. The mind is attached to knowing. It wants to understand, conceptualise, predict and control, and it will accept a bad explanation over no explanation every time. Because when the mind knows, it believes it is in control.

Every hindrance you will meet in practice (Stage 4) is one of these two knots pulling. Restlessness, pain, boredom and sleepiness are the comfort knot. Doubt, analysis, planning and the search for the right technique are the knowing knot. Seeking comfort with the body and knowing with the mind, we remain like herd animals: craving pleasure, avoiding pain, endlessly responding to stimulus, endlessly doing, and unaware of the dimension of being.

The alternative is a phrase worth memorising: present without choosing. To be fully attentive to what is happening, without the mediation of conditioning, without concepts, without controlling or manipulating what arises, is to be present without choosing, and therefore without a chooser. The knots loosen not by force but by being neither obeyed nor fought.

In the pursuit of learning, every day something is acquired. In the pursuit of the Tao, every day something is dropped.

Go deeperConditions of no escape

Why do retreats exist? Because in ordinary life the character always has an exit. When practice becomes uncomfortable, there is a phone, a snack, a conversation, a task. Each exit is a socket. A retreat is engineered to remove the exits, to create conditions of no escape for the ego, so that what it has been avoiding has nowhere to go but up into awareness. This is not cruelty. It is the fan being kept unplugged long enough to slow.

The same principle can be built into a day: a fixed sitting time that is not negotiated, a period without media, a commitment not to move during a sit. The point is never to brutalise the body or mind (see the note on productive and unproductive pain in Stage 4), but to stop offering the pattern its usual escape routes.

Yoga's classical texts describe the goal as chitta vritti nirodha: the cessation of the whirlpool of the mind. A whirlpool is not a thing; it is water going round. Stop feeding it and it is simply water. The related terms vasana (latent tendency) and klesha (affliction) name the same momentum from different angles: the deep unconscious patterns that produce the limited sense of "I" and the suffering that attends it.

Buddhism: sankhara is the fourth aggregate and also the second link in the chain of dependent origination, the "formations" that arise from ignorance and drive rebirth, moment to moment. To burn them up is the literal meaning of jhana (Stage 6).

Hinduism: samskara, vasana and karma form a single vocabulary of momentum. The Gita's answer is action without attachment to the fruit, which is precisely refusing to plug the fan back in while still letting the blades do what they do.

Taoism: the Tao Te Ching's daily "dropping" is the fan analogy stated as a practice. Wu wei, non-forcing, is not passivity; it is acting without the extra layer of grasping that creates new channels.

Christianity: Augustine described habit as a chain forged link by link until it holds you fast, which is a samskara in Latin. The contemplative instruction "be still, and know" (Psalm 46) is the instruction to stop reaching for the socket.

Sufism: the disciplining of the nafs, and the great saying "die before you die", both describe letting the momentum of the self run down while still alive, so that what remains can be seen.

Practice: catching the hand on the socket

All day, in ordinary life. No extra time needed. The first week, aim to catch it three times a day.

  1. Pick one recurring small reaction you know you have: irritation at a particular sound, reaching for the phone when bored, a flare of defensiveness when corrected.
  2. The next time it fires, do not stop it and do not follow it. Instead, find the sensation in the body that came first: the tightening, the heat, the drop in the stomach. This is the plug being lifted toward the socket.
  3. Stay with the raw sensation for ten full breaths without adding a story. Let it be exactly as strong as it is.
  4. Notice what happens to the reaction when it is felt rather than fed. Usually it crests and passes like a wave, larger at first, then smaller each time.
  5. At the end of the day, note once, briefly: how many times did the hand reach for the socket, and how many times was it caught? No judgement. Counting is enough.

You are not trying to become someone who never reacts. You are letting a groove fill in by not running water down it. Each time it is caught, the channel is a little shallower.

Stage 4 of 10

Sitting

There are hundreds of meditation techniques and they all do the same two things. Once you see what those two things are, the technique matters much less, and what comes up while you do it matters much more.

In plain words

Every technique combines concentration (staying with one thing) and surrender (not reacting to what arises). Together they interrupt the habit pattern of the mind. When the pattern is interrupted, its defences come up as hindrances: pain, sleepiness, restlessness, doubt, a busy mind. These are not obstacles. They are the exact material that needs to be made conscious, arriving on schedule.

What every technique has in common

Breath-watching, body scanning, mantra, gazing, chanting: strip any of them down and you find a focusing element and a letting-go element. Concentration and equanimity. Attention and surrender. When these two are present together, the brain's neuroplasticity is at its highest; neurons that fire together wire together, and what is being wired is the capacity to be present without reacting.

The path is therefore a paradox, and it is better to know that at the start than to discover it as a disappointment. From one side there is no path: the awareness you want to awaken to is already here, and there are no steps to arrive at the present moment. From the other side there clearly is a path: identification is progressively loosened, and things happen in a recognisable order. There is a path until there isn't. Techniques are how the conditioned self interrupts its own pattern, creating conditions in which the flip is more likely. When the flip comes, you see that it was always the character doing something it thought it needed to do.

An old image: a technique is a thorn used to remove a thorn. Once the first thorn is out, you throw both away. Hold onto the technique, keep repeating it, believe it is special, and it becomes another layer of the self-structure, another control mechanism. Ramana Maharshi's warning is blunt: to let the mind run the practice is to make the thief the chief of police.

Three words that are not the same word

Concentration is the camera. It can be turned toward the breath, a sound, a point between the eyebrows. It can be narrow (air at the nostrils) or wide (the whole body expanding and contracting). Turning toward and setting the width: that is concentration.

Attention is the continuity of staying with the object through time. Attention span. Patanjali's image is oil poured from a jar: an unbroken stream, not drops. Concentration chooses the object; attention keeps it.

Awareness is neither. It is what the camera and its continuity appear in. It has no direction, no width and no quality; it is not something you turn or hold. Krishnamurti called it choiceless for exactly this reason: the mind chooses where to point, and awareness is simply aware of that choosing. When these pages say "rest as awareness", they never mean "point your awareness somewhere". They mean stop pointing.

The eye of the bird

In the Mahabharata, the archery master Drona sets a wooden bird in a tree and has his students take aim at its eye. As each draws the bow he asks: what do you see? One sees the bird, the tree, the other students, his own arrow. Dismissed. Another sees, in his mind's eye, the arrow entering the eye. Closer, but no. One cleverly says there is no bird. Nice try. Last comes Arjuna, and when asked what he sees he says: I see only the eye of the bird.

That is single-pointedness, and it is the key to the whole progressive path. When you take aim at your meditation object, there is nothing else. Not looking around, not reviewing how it is going, not deciding whether this is the right technique. Only the breath. The same instruction appears in St John of the Cross's ascent of Mount Carmel: climb straight up, looking neither left nor right, not stopping to pick the flowers and not frightened off by the creatures on the path. And it appears in Taoism as the reason the method works: chi follows consciousness, so when consciousness is gathered into one point, energy gathers with it, and the burning-off of conditioning begins in earnest.

It does not much matter what the object is. Breath, energy in the body, the space between the eyebrows, a chant: each is a bridge into the same field of aliveness the yogis call prana. Watch the breath long enough and you become the breath; chant long enough and you become the sound. The watcher and the watched merge. Breath is the bridge, energy is the bridge, and every road leads to the same water.

Hindrances are the material

Apply single-pointed attention and surrender for more than a few minutes and the mind's defences arrive. Tiredness, a sudden need to move, an itch, doubt about the whole enterprise, a mind running scenarios like a television left on. The classic error is to treat these as things going wrong. They are the self-structure showing you exactly where it is contracted and unconscious. A hindrance is an invitation to bring consciousness to some part of your being. Even the question "how do I overcome sleepiness?" contains the mistake, because the little self that wants to overcome it is part of the sleepiness.

There are two ways to meet any hindrance, and both arrive at the same place.

Inhabit the pattern

Take sleepiness. It has a shape: heaviness around the eyes, a particular feeling in the head, a change in the breath, a slumping or swaying in the posture. Bring the entire pattern into view and inhabit it. How does it change over time? Is it continuous or does it come in waves? What does the breath do when it arrives? A hindrance can only take you over while it runs in the shadows. Fully seen, it becomes intimate, then it loses its power, and then it can drop. This is the phenomenological approach, and the body scan (below) is its most developed form.

Inquire directly

Alternatively, when tiredness arises, ask: to whom does this tiredness belong? Who is tired? Clearly the mind-body is tired. But am I the mind-body? There is an awareness that is aware of the tired body; who is that? If the inquiry lands, there is an instant flip: people who were exhausted a moment before light up, because the tiredness is still there and it has nothing to do with them. This is the direct approach, and Stage 7 is devoted to it.

Which to use is a matter of intuition and of where you are on the path. The self-structure is a candle; it does not matter which end you burn.

Two techniques worth learning

Labelling thoughts

For the mind that will not stop. Every thought is either an image (visual), an inner voice (auditory), or both. Sit with your anchor, and each time a thought pulls you away, simply note it: "visual", "auditory", or "both". Do not enter the story; do not argue with it; do not evaluate it. Note the type and return. Something surprising happens: even a heavy, frightening thought, once noted as "auditory", pops like a balloon. Repeated for a session, the technique reveals recurring patterns and drains their energy. You are using the mind to break a habit of the mind.

The body scan

For samskaras held in the body. Move attention slowly and systematically through the body, part by part, head to feet and back, observing whatever sensation is present in each part with equanimity: no preference for pleasant over unpleasant, no lingering, no skipping. Where there is nothing to feel, note that too and move on. The scan brings attention to the second skandha, raw sensation, exactly where the unconscious knots are stored, and equanimity is what lets them release. It is the same principle as inhabiting a hindrance, applied to the whole body.

Productive and unproductive pain

The invitation to sit without reacting has a limit, and it is important to know it. Productive pain is karmic: aches that only exist while sitting, the "squirm", restlessness, discomfort that is the pattern of resistance itself, held in the body and rising to be felt. The way out of it is through. Unproductive pain is the intelligence of the body reporting a real problem: an old injury, a nerve, a warning sign such as light-headedness, dry throat and vision closing in. That is not to be pushed through. Adjust the posture, use a cushion, switch to walking meditation, stop. Never brutalise the vessel.

The same distinction holds psychologically. There is a middle way between blasting through and babying yourself: move beyond the comfort zone, but not so far that the nervous system goes into overwhelm. Sweating, shaking, a sense of losing control that has no equanimity in it at all: these are signs to step back, ground the body (a barefoot walk outside works), and return when stable. Someone once asked a monk what he was doing in Las Vegas; he said anyone can be a monk on a mountain top. A noisy apartment or a difficult life is training with weights on. But weights can be too heavy, and only you can feel where that line is.

Go deeperDirect path and progressive path

Teachers tend to fall into one of two camps. The direct-path teachers point relentlessly at the awareness that is here now, with no on-ramp: you either get it or you do not. Krishnamurti dismissed techniques altogether because it was so obvious to him that awareness is choiceless and already present. The progressive-path teachers give a method and hold that doing it faithfully leads to liberation; the Vipassana tradition of the body scan is a pure example. Both are right, and both arrive at the same samadhi.

The Buddha taught at every level, which is why his teaching has both. The famous Flower Sermon was pure direct pointing: he held up a white flower in silence, and one student in the whole assembly understood. Everyone else needed intermediary steps. Ramana Maharshi's image was of dry kindling that ignites at once versus wet wood that needs time to dry. Most people are wet wood for a while, and then are not. A reasonable approach is to work with a technique until concentration and presence are established, and then let the technique fall away into simply sitting, simply being.

The yogic term for the last three limbs of Patanjali's eightfold system, practised together, is samyama: dharana (concentration, the effortful part), dhyana (absorption, the surrendering part) and samadhi (where doing and non-doing merge). Every technique is a version of the first two, aimed at the third.

Go deeperPractical questions that always come up

Lying down. Generally discouraged, because almost everyone falls asleep, and because sitting is what allows energy to build and break through. There is one rule in most Zen halls and it is no lying down. The exception is someone who reliably stays awake lying down and is integrating a strong energetic release; then lying can let the energy move.

Moving. If the body wants to move, ask who is moving. Sometimes it is the conditioned mind seeking relief and comfort; sometimes it is energy beginning to move the body on its own (see kriyas in the field guide). Learn to tell them apart. Dissipating the build-up through movement brings relief but never the breakthrough; there is an alchemy that happens only when the little self reaches the point where something has to give.

Noise. Explore dropping the concept of the sound ("annoying dog") and feeling the raw vibration as it arrives. If it is truly overwhelming the nervous system, use sound or ambient noise; otherwise treat it as weights on the equanimity muscle.

Controlling the breath. A little settling breathwork at the start is fine, but notice when the controller has taken over. Let the body be breathed. What happens when the one managing the breath relaxes?

Stiff hips, restless legs, a back that will not sit. Two years of ordinary stretching yoga or qigong has made the difference for many people who could not sit long enough for energy to rise. This is not spiritual; it is plumbing.

Practice: a twenty-minute sit

Daily. Same time, same place if you can. A timer, a straight spine, eyes closed or lowered. Do not move for the full twenty minutes unless real pain requires it.

  1. Aim. Choose one object for the whole sit: the breath at the nostrils, the rise and fall of the belly, or the point between the eyebrows. Only the eye of the bird.
  2. Stay. Keep attention on the object like oil pouring from a jar. When it breaks, notice the break without comment and return. A hundred returns is a hundred repetitions of the skill, not a hundred failures.
  3. Label. When a thought pulls you off, note it silently as "visual", "auditory" or "both", then return. Do not enter the story.
  4. Inhabit. When a hindrance arrives (sleepiness, pain, restlessness), turn toward it for a while. Find its full pattern in the body and breath. Then either return to the object, or ask to whom it belongs.
  5. Scan. For the last five minutes, sweep attention slowly from the crown to the feet, part by part, meeting whatever sensation is there with no preference. Then rest with no object at all for the final minute: nothing to aim at, nothing to do.

If light-headedness, dry throat, ringing or tunnel vision come on, stop: that is the body's intelligence, not a samskara. If you finish and the mind says "that was a bad sit", remember that the murkiest sessions are often the ones that do the most.

Stage 5 of 10

No Preferences

A seventh-century Zen poem opens with a line that contains the entire path: the Great Way is not difficult for those who have no preferences. Everything else in this stage is commentary on that sentence.

In plain words

The mind divides everything into what it likes and what it dislikes, then spends its life chasing one and fleeing the other. That dividing is the disease. Not the things themselves, not the sensations, not even the thoughts: the setting of "for" against "against". Drop that, even for a moment, and the Zen poem says everything becomes clear and undisguised.

The disease of the mind

The Hsin Hsin Ming, attributed to Sengcan, the third patriarch of Zen, is a short poem on "faith in mind" (or, in some renderings, "trust in the heart"). Its diagnosis is exact. To set what you like against what you dislike is the disease of the mind. Make the smallest distinction, it says, and heaven and earth are set infinitely apart. If you wish to see the truth, hold no opinions for or against anything.

Notice what the poem does not say. It does not say stop sensing, stop thinking, or stop noticing differences. Coarse and fine, movement and rest, self and other are all allowed to appear. What ends is the reflex of grabbing one pole and pushing away the other. The poem is also careful to close the escape hatch that clever meditators reach for: trying to stop activity in order to achieve passivity, it warns, only fills you with activity. Even to be attached to the idea of enlightenment is to go astray. And most pointedly: to seek Mind (the true self, capital M) with the discriminating mind is the greatest of all mistakes. You cannot use the tool of preference to find what is prior to preference.

Read in the context of Stages 2 and 3, this is a precise instruction. The fourth skandha, the habit layer of craving and aversion, is where the "I" is thickest and where the fan gets plugged in. The Great Way is simply: stop feeding that layer. Let everything within the field of change arise and pass without dividing it into this and that.

When thought objects vanish, the thinking subject vanishes. Things are objects because of the subject; the mind is such because of things.

Hsin Hsin Ming

That couplet is the Buddhist statement of what Stage 2 called the witness and the witnessed arising together. Take away the preferring, and there is no longer a preferer. The poem ends with the phrase that names the whole project: with no preferences, "life in beginner's mind is possible", and in that mind there is no yesterday, no tomorrow, no today.

The way of water

Half a world and a thousand years before Sengcan, the Tao Te Ching said the same thing in the language of nature. Heaven and earth have no preference. The highest good is like water: it benefits all things and does not compete, because it is content to settle in the low places that people despise. The most yielding thing in the world overcomes the most rigid. Thirty spokes meet at the hub, but it is the empty hole at the centre that lets the wheel turn; clay is shaped into a cup, but it is the emptiness inside that holds the water. Stillness benefits more than action, silence more than words.

The Taoist practice that follows is wu wei, usually translated "non-action" and better understood as non-forcing. It is not passivity. Water is never passive; it is simply never in conflict with the shape of things. A mind without preferences moves the way water moves: around the rock rather than into it, always arriving at the sea. The current from Stage 1 is the Tao, and preference is the only thing that ever fights it.

Maybe

A farmer in a small village owned a fine horse, and the villagers said how lucky he was. One day the horse broke out and ran off into the hills. The villagers came to console him: what bad luck. The farmer said, "Maybe. I don't know." A few days later the horse returned, leading a band of wild horses. Wonderful, said the villagers, what good fortune. "Maybe. I don't know." The farmer's son tried to tame one of the wild horses, was thrown and shattered his leg. Terrible, said the villagers. "Maybe. I don't know." The next week the king's army came through, conscripting every young man for a war, and left the boy with the broken leg behind.

The farmer is not indifferent; he loves his son and works his land. What he has given up is the belief that the mind is qualified to judge events as good or bad. This is the "don't-know mind" that every contemplative tradition prizes, and Socrates's version was the foundation of Western philosophy: I know only that I know nothing. Applied to the villagers' running commentary in your own head, it dissolves an astonishing amount of suffering in a single word.

Don't we have to fight evil?

This question always comes, and it deserves a straight answer. The question assumes someone who knows what is evil. Every war in history has had two sides who each believed the other was the evil one. The point is not that nothing is wrong; it is that the mind's certainty about it is the same machinery of preference the poem is describing, now at the scale of nations. The Bhagavad Gita opens with exactly this dilemma. Arjuna, facing a battle against his own relatives, cannot see how to choose. Krishna, who represents the awakened Self, does not tell him which side is right. He tells him to let go of the doer: act, but without claiming the action; let awakened consciousness go into battle in your place.

Compassion is not something the character achieves by adopting a moral code. It is what remains when the illusion of separation thins, because finding out who you are is also finding out who everyone else is. Moral codes, then laws, are the facsimiles a society builds when direct contact has been lost. They are necessary in their place, but they are downstream of the thing itself. Repair the split at its root and compassion is not a rule; it is simply how life moves through an undivided vessel.

Go deeperQuestioning a thought instead of obeying it

Preferences do not arrive naked. They come dressed as thoughts, and thoughts come dressed as facts. A practical bridge between "hold no opinions" and daily life is to question the thought before believing it. The four questions Byron Katie built her method around are a clean version: Is it true? Can I absolutely know it is true? How do I react, and what happens, when I believe that thought? Who would I be without it? Do this a few dozen times and a pattern emerges: believing the thought produces suffering and identification; dropping it frees energy. Eventually you know the game well enough not to believe the next thought at all, because you know where that road goes. Krishnamurti put the whole thing in seven words: "I don't mind what happens." And in four: no thoughts, no problems.

This is not a method for attacking the mind. The mind is already at war with itself; it is already the struggle and the one who appears to be struggling. Questioning a thought is not more fighting; it is withdrawing belief, which is the fuel.

Practice: the farmer's mind

One day, from waking to sleep. Then one day a week.

  2. Notice the feeling that follows. Usually a small release, sometimes a flare of resistance from a mind that very much wants to know. Feel the resistance in the body without arguing with it.
  3. Choose one strongly held opinion from the day (about a person, a situation, yourself). Ask: is it true? Can I absolutely know it is true? What happens when I believe it? Who would I be without it?
  4. For the last ten minutes of the day, sit and let every thought come and go without picking any up. Not suppressing, not following. Just not preferring.

You will still make decisions, cross the road, and refuse what should be refused. Preference is not the same as discernment. What is being dropped is the charge, the "this must not be", not the ability to act.

Stage 6 of 10

Absorption

As sitting deepens, the mind passes through recognisable stages of absorption, and some of them are the most beautiful experiences a human being can have. That is precisely the danger. This stage maps the territory and names the one mistake almost everyone makes in it.

In plain words

The Pali word jhana (Sanskrit dhyana, from which come Chan and Zen) traces back to a root meaning "to burn up". Meditation burns up conditioning, and as it burns, energy is freed and consciousness changes, layer by layer. These states are real, they are signs of progress, and none of them is who you are. Mistaking a state for your true nature is the one mistake that keeps the whole game of "I got it, I lost it" going.

What burns, and in what order

Here is the general shape of the progression, held loosely. It is far better to discover these things in your own sitting than to hunt for them with a checklist.

The hindrances. At the start, the mind is a whirlpool of preference: craving, aversion, restlessness, doubt. This is dukkha, unsatisfactoriness, as a felt state. Practice here is mostly the work of Stage 4, cultivating enough equanimity that the hindrances stop being hindrances and become the way in.

Energy breaks through. When reaction to preference slows, something gives. Energy the Pali texts call piti (rapture, or simply freed prana) rises, and the felt tone shifts from struggle to ease: from dukkha to sukha, satisfactoriness. This is the first jhana, and it feels good. It can arrive as tingling, warmth, waves, light, geometric colour, sound, or a change in the state of consciousness itself.

Letting go of the good feeling. A new game begins immediately: the mind learns that this can be produced and starts preferring it. So the next thing to release is sukha itself. Anyone who plays the sensation game long enough discovers it is endless; states arise and pass, and chasing them is samsara with a nicer decor. The insight of impermanence, anicca, begins here as a felt fact rather than a doctrine. Letting the states come and go is a deeper equanimity, and another layer of mind drops.

Single-pointedness. The small waves settle. The mind becomes one-pointed, energy builds, and there is a stage, often the fourth jhana, where the part of the mind that distinguishes pain from pleasure goes quiet. People report tireless energy, as if they could sit forever. This is the end of the "material" jhanas, the ones still connected to body and sensation.

Mind and body drop away. Keep sitting and the body-mind falls off. The "immaterial" jhanas are rarely discussed because there is little point: consciousness experienced as infinite; then states the mind cannot even frame, such as "neither perception nor non-perception". Very subtle layers of self drop here. Dogen's definition of meditation, "to drop off body and mind", refers to this.

Hold all of this lightly. If it becomes a set of boxes to tick ("got the first one, second one covered"), it has become dharma as another possession of the ego. A menu describes the meal; nobody is nourished by eating the menu. The map is useful only so that when the territory appears you are not frightened, and not fooled.

Samadhi with a seed

The deepest of the technique-produced states is the union experience: being one with everything. In the yoga sutras it is savikalpa or sabija samadhi, samadhi "with a seed", because a seed of the self-structure survives inside it and will sprout again. In Buddhist terms it is the fruit of the material jhanas. It is temporary, as every state is. And it is the peak experience of a human life.

Which is why it should come with a warning. Consider a common story. A beginner at a meditation retreat, who still fully believes they are the character, has this experience. They walk out under the stars and are the stars; the tree is them; everything is them. The character thinks: this is it, this is what the Buddha and Christ had, I did it. Then, as every state does, it fades. And in the fading, the seeker is born: a desperate hunger to get it back, which drives years of sutras, techniques, teachers, retreats, and a growing pile of knowledge that only moves them further from it. The seeker finally fails, completely, in some place with no escape, having tried every trick. The mind is humbled; it realises it knows nothing about its true nature. And in that failure, with nothing left for the character to do, the conditions are right for the flip.

The lesson is not that union is bad. It is that it is far better to awaken to what you are before having the union experience, because the experience, arriving to someone who still believes they are the character, almost guarantees the creation of a seeker.

The one mistake

There is really only one mistake on this path, and it perpetuates everything else: mistaking a state, any state, for the truth of who you are. The state is phenomena. It is in time; it comes and goes; the mind can remember it, or thinks it can. What it actually remembers is the energy, the feelings, the light, the sense of expansion. It cannot remember the awareness that was present, because awareness is not an experience. It is what the experience appeared in, and it is here now, unchanged, while you read this sentence.

So when the mind says "I had it and I lost it", ask what "it" was. If it was the beautiful phenomena, then yes: gone, as all phenomena go. But the one who knew the phenomena was not part of the phenomena. The awareness in the peak experience is the awareness here now. Nothing about it has changed; only the show around it changed. Recognise that, and there is nothing to get back.

Picture the whole of human experience as a line. At one end, total union, love, bliss. At the other, total identification, separation, hell. Every moment of a life is somewhere on that line. Who experiences the entire line? That one is what these stages are trying to reveal, and it is not at either end. When identity flips from the character travelling the line to the awareness the line appears in, there is a peace that is not contingent on where the character currently is. It is not the joy at the happy end of the line. It is something of a different order: the relief of knowing what you are.

Practice: who knows this state?

Whenever a notable state arises in sitting, pleasant or unpleasant. Also once, deliberately, with a memory of the best moment you have ever had in meditation.

  1. Let the state be fully present. Do not dampen it and do not chase it. Have the complete experience of it; without grasping, you experience it more, not less.
  2. Notice the mind's first move: a flicker of "yes, keep this" or "no, make this stop". That flicker is the fourth skandha, the seed. Feel it and let it be.
  3. Ask, without straining for an answer: who is the knower of this state? Not what the state is, but what it is appearing to.
  4. Notice that whatever answer arises is itself another state, another thing known. Let it go too. Rest as the knowing.
  5. When the state fades, and it will, notice whether the knowing faded with it. Check honestly. That which did not fade is what this whole path is about.

The mind will complain that it cannot find the knower. Correct: the knower is not findable as an object. The mind's failure here is not your failure. It is the beginning of the mind learning its limits.

Stage 7 of 10

Who Am I?

The stages so far have described the mechanism from the outside. Direct inquiry goes in through the front door: it turns attention toward the one who is asking, and lets the mind fail to find an answer. The silence where the answer should be is the point.

In plain words

Every thought, feeling and sensation is something you are aware of. Inquiry asks what is aware. The mind cannot answer, because every answer it produces is one more thing you are aware of. When the mind is finally humbled, when it stops producing candidates and gives up, what remains is not an answer but a direct experience. This is the only game you win by giving up.

The I-thought

Look at what the word "I" actually points to when it arises in you. It is attached to a body, a name, a set of preferences, a history. Ramana Maharshi's whole teaching rested on tracing this "I"-thought back to its source: not the "I" that says "I want" or "I am tired", but what that "I" is standing on. His instruction was to ask who am I?, not as a question expecting words but as a turning of attention toward the asker.

A working definition that makes inquiry sharper: the I-thought is the resistance to what is. In any moment there are thoughts, sensations, perceptions, arising and passing. If there is a preference inside any of it, a "this should be otherwise", that is the I-thought. Everything else is just the show. So the inquiry is not "which of these experiences is me?" but "who is the one resisting?", and then, "who is aware of that one?"

Do not expect to figure it out. The distinction between the false "I" and the true is not something a third "I" works out by analysis. It is a stripping away. Identification dissolves, and with it the question. When the questioning mind drops, there is no residue of doubt, and nothing to distinguish.

Tell me who you are

A structured form of inquiry, developed in the 1960s and since used in intensive retreats around the world, does the inquiry in pairs and can produce awakening (kensho) with surprising speed. It is called the dyad. It works because saying what arises, out loud, to a neutral witness, makes unconscious resistance visible in a way that solitary sitting often does not.

Two people sit facing each other. One is the witness; the other is the inquirer. The witness delivers the instruction: Tell me who you are. This is not a question. It is an imperative, a command to communicate yourself, with your whole being, not with a definition. The inquirer then does three things at once:

  1. Intends to directly experience their true nature, beyond the thinking mind. Not to describe it; to experience it.
  2. Notices whatever arises as a result of that intention: a contraction in the chest, a memory, a feeling of blankness, fear, laughter, an opinion, a story, silence.
  3. Communicates it to the witness, authentically and without editing. Whatever it is. Not a performance of spirituality, and not a weather report of mundane sensations either, but the actual content of the mind as it defends, deflects, softens and opens.

The witness does nothing. No nodding, no "mm-hmm", no facial commentary, no advice, no prompting. Pure neutral presence, receiving. After five minutes, the roles swap. That is the whole technique.

What happens in practice is that the mind searches for an answer and reports everything it finds: "I'm a mother", "I'm tired", "I'm awareness" (a concept), "I don't know", "this is pointless". Each report is a piece of the self-structure being cleared, spoken and released. Doing it, people are also told to scrutinise every "I", "me" and "my" they say, to see whether the word is referring to the character or to what is aware of the character. Eventually the mind runs out. The search fails. And in the failure, the inquirer is simply here, wordlessly conveying what they are to another human being. Some meet themselves for the first time in that moment.

This is the only game you win by giving up. You give up control, you give up knowing, you surrender, and in the stillness of the surrender you experience the truth.

On the dyad

The mind has no place here

Two kinds of trouble arise reliably in inquiry, and they are the same trouble.

The first is striving. The question becomes a torment: I must find out who I am, I cannot be happy until I do, and time is running out. Notice that the striving is the movement of the mind, wanting something other than what is, and the mind does not even know what it wants. That striving is exactly what must relax for stillness to be there. The relief when it finally does is enormous, and it is the relief of discovering that what was being sought was here the whole time. If sitting quietly and being present is easy but the question makes you tense, then be present, and look at who is present. That is the inquiry.

The second is the gap. Someone who had a glimpse years ago says: I recognise awareness now, but it does not feel like it did then; there is a gap and I am trying to cross it. There is no gap. The mind remembers the phenomena that accompanied the glimpse, and is comparing this moment's phenomena unfavourably. The awareness in the glimpse and the awareness reading this are one and the same. Let the memory go, and there is nothing to cross.

Both troubles resolve when the mind learns the one thing it has to learn in this whole process: its own limitation. It cannot do this. Its actions in this domain are futile, all of them. Once it truly gets that, seeking ends, not by force but by the seeker seeing its own irrelevance. And being, which was never absent, is obvious.

Go deeperHow to hold the dyad well

No prompting. An untrained witness who starts guiding ("but tell me who you are, tell me about awareness") breaks the technique. Prompting is a skill for facilitators who have themselves awakened and are clear. Between friends or partners, hold pure neutrality and let the imperative do the work. The technique works without any facilitator at all.

Clearing before answering. If what arises is dark, frightening or "not spiritual", that is precisely what to report. The dyad is a clearing process first. What blocks the direct experience must surface and be spoken before the experience is available. An inquirer who feels a demon-like energy arise and reports it is doing it right; a witness who says "no, tell me about awareness" is not.

Weather reports. Reporting "my knee itches, a car went by, I feel neutral" for five minutes is the mind on a loop. Feel the frustration and pointlessness of that, report that, and look for what is being held just under the surface. Go toward feeling, not analysis, almost like a child playing.

With a spouse. Dyads between partners can be beautiful and can be volatile, because the character has twenty years of material with this particular witness. Keep the roles strict, keep the witness silent, and treat what comes up as clearing, not as a conversation to continue afterwards.

Solo. Without a partner, sit and receive the imperative from yourself, or simply become aware of being. Intend to directly experience; notice what arises; let it be fully felt and cleared; return. It is the same process without the mirror.

Practice: solo inquiry, then the dyad

Solo: ten minutes daily for a week. Dyad: forty minutes with a partner, in five-minute turns, once a week or more. Read the "Go deeper" notes on holding the dyad first.

  1. Settle. Sit. Let the breath be natural. Let the seeking relax; you are not going anywhere.
  2. Receive the imperative. Say inwardly, or hear from your partner: tell me who you are. Let it land as a command to be, not a question to answer.
  3. Intend. Intend to directly experience what you are, prior to thought. Do not describe; turn toward.
  4. Notice and communicate. Whatever arises (a tightening, a memory, "I don't know", a story, fear, joy, blankness), say it aloud to the witness, or note it clearly if alone. Do not edit and do not perform. Then return to the intention.
  5. Watch the "I". Each time you say "I", "me" or "my", check: which one? The character, or what is aware of the character?
  6. Let the mind fail. When it runs out of answers, do not rescue it. Stay in the silence where the answer should be. Convey yourself from there, without words.
  7. Swap after five minutes. As witness: receive everything, offer nothing.

If a strong release comes (tears, shaking, a wave of energy), let it. If overwhelm comes and equanimity is completely gone, stop, ground the body, and return another day. The technique works; trust it and keep going.

Stage 8 of 10

Fear and the Shadow

At a certain depth the work stops being pleasant. The self-structure, feeling its grip loosen, produces a fear that seems to have no object. This is not a sign that something has gone wrong. It is the sign that inquiry has reached the root.

In plain words

Fear is contracted life energy. When the little self relaxes its grip, the same energy that was fear becomes aliveness; nothing is added or removed. The path does not go around the dark parts of the psyche; it goes through them, and the hero's task in every mythology is not to slay the dragon but to ride it.

Primordial fear

Practitioners who reach this point describe the same thing: a fear that is not of anything. Not fear of a snake or a deadline, but fear itself, a feeling of not being in control, of imminent dissolution. The mind rushes to supply objects: I will go mad, I will faint, I will lose my family, I will end up homeless, I will die. None of these is the fear. They are stories the mind wraps around a sensation it cannot explain.

What is actually happening is that the "I"-thought is giving way. Teachers who have taken students through this consider it good news: it means the work is correct and has reached the depths. Nobody gets an easy ride at the root of the self-structure. When it gets real, it gets real. And the moment fear of this kind arises is the single best moment to inquire: to whom does this fear belong? Who is watching this character go through its letting-go?

It helps to reassure the character. What feels like death is not death. The conditioned self is moving from the driver's seat to the back seat. It will still be part of the team; it will still get to play. Over time it discovers that what comes through when it stops steering is better than anything it could do alone: less predictable, more alive. The phrase "die before you die" is ancient and accurate, but it frightens the character, so it is worth translating: let go of control before your body dies, and see what is left.

The roller coaster

Consider what happens on a large roller coaster. Climbing the first hill, you can feel fear building in the body. There are two ways to ride. In the first, at the top, something flips: you become the witness, the emptiness riding the coaster, watching the drama from a remove. It is peaceful, and it is not really riding. In the second, something gives way entirely: every sense opens, the energy opens, the emptiness is still there but so is all of the fullness, and the ride is life itself riding, connected to the whole park. Emptiness riding is a real stage. Non-dual riding is where it is going: form and emptiness together, fear and aliveness revealed as one energy.

If you hold on, the energy contracts and there is only survival. If you let go, the identical energy is bliss. It is the same with cold water: plunge in tense and it is shock; let go into it and, in time, the nervous system learns that the shock was the resistance. Practitioners who have been through the primordial fear many times report that the same energetic movement that once terrified them now simply passes through as bliss. The body learns that letting go does not mean oblivion.

Hope and fear are one thing

Fear cannot be dropped on its own, because it is half of a pair. Hope is craving projected into the future (may this happen); fear is aversion projected into the future (may this not happen). They are the fourth skandha, wearing two masks. The "I"-thought is strongest exactly here, in hoping and fearing, and this is not a flaw in the design; it is the pulse of expansion and contraction that runs through everything, the in-breath and out-breath, yin and yang, Shiva and Shakti.

Over the gate of hell in Dante's Inferno are the words "abandon all hope, you who enter here". Read as instruction rather than threat, it is exact. Hell is the ego structure. To get through it you abandon hope, and you abandon fear, because they are the same mechanism. What is left when both are gone is not despair. It is presence. Seen clearly, fear is like the Wizard of Oz: a terrifying facade with nothing behind the curtain. Or the classic snake in the rope: frightening until you see it is a rope, and then there is nothing to be frightened of, and there never was.

The cave

Joseph Campbell, who spent his life mapping the hero's journey across cultures, summarised its turning point in one sentence: where we had thought to find an abomination, we shall find a god; where we had thought to slay another, we shall slay ourselves; where we had thought to travel outward, we shall come to the centre of our own existence.

The clearest modern telling is in the first Star Wars trilogy. Luke, training with Yoda, is sent into a dark cave. Yoda tells him he will not need his weapon. He takes it anyway, meets Darth Vader, strikes him down, and the mask falls open to show Luke's own face. Every real journey has this scene. The thing you feared most in the dark turns out to be a disowned part of you.

Carl Jung called these disowned parts the shadow, and he was unambiguous about the direction of the work: "One does not become enlightened by imagining figures of light, but by making the darkness conscious." And: the hero's main feat is to overcome the monster of darkness, "the long hoped-for triumph of consciousness over the unconscious". Much of modern spirituality is about imagining figures of light. It does not work, because whatever is left unconscious continues to run, and we call the results fate, or karma. Make it conscious and it is simply part of you.

Ride the dragon

Our stories have regressed. The old myths knew the dragon must be integrated; the new ones mostly have it slain, light conquering dark, good conquering evil. Look at the oldest images of the Buddha, or of Patanjali, in the ancient temples: they sit surrounded by nagas, serpents, sometimes seven of them fanning out behind the awakened one. The serpents are not being fought. They are the dimensions of the unconscious, integrated, at attention, part of the whole. Modern iconography dropped them, and something was lost.

The serpent has been demonised for two thousand years: the spiral, the feminine, the manifested world, the power of becoming. Wars have been fought and people burned out of fear of it. But that power is simply nature, life itself, and only the ego ever goes to war with it. The story of the fall makes sense read this way: Lucifer, the most beautiful angel, splits off, "rather to rule in hell than serve in heaven". That is the ego, exactly: my will, not Thine, as if they were two. The fallen angel is the angel that became an ego. The demon and the angel are one energy; reject the demon and you have rejected the angel too.

This is why the shadow returns in relationships, synchronicities and triggers. Whatever you push away in yourself shows up in the world as a mirror. The person who repels you is showing you an exile. Integration does not mean acting the shadow out, and it does not mean befriending everyone who carries it. It means noticing what in yourself is being resisted, feeling it fully, and letting it come back into the one.

Two horses

The Bhagavad Gita pictures the person as a chariot: the horses are the senses and desires, the reins the mind, the charioteer the discerning intelligence, and the passenger the Self. Plato's Phaedrus has a sharper version with two horses. The black horse, eros, pulls down toward the material, toward sensation and pleasure and the world. The white horse, thumos, pulls upward toward the spiritual, toward truth, toward what is higher. The charioteer must drive both.

The teaching is in what Plato does not say. He does not say kill the black horse. Kill it and you have lost half your power; the chariot cannot move. Let the white horse run unchecked and you neglect the body and the world, and the chariot goes off the road just as surely. The horses are the skandhas, and energy follows consciousness: make them conscious, harness both, and the chariot goes where the current is going. The material and the spiritual are not enemies. They are the team.

Not everyone feels this, and it is not required, but for those who do the pattern is consistent. The habitual centre of "I" sits around the brow, the third eye. As practice deepens, energy moves up from there toward the crown, and the sense of "I" has to give way for that movement to complete. If the little "I" is clinging, that is the moment fear arrives. With repetition, the same movement becomes a small, blissful event rather than a terror. Kundalini teachings describe this as the awakening of evolutionary potential through the body's centres, the chakras; the crown is where the personal current rejoins the whole.

Some traditions use literal darkness. A darkness retreat, several days without sun or moon, removes the circadian control mechanisms of duality. After three or four days something changes; the body may release its own visionary chemistry, and there is no longer dark and light but, in the words of those who have done it, only luminous emptiness, a waking dream that goes on forever. It is the cave, taken literally.

Jung also spoke of the collective unconscious. In deep clearing, what surfaces sometimes does not feel personal: generational, archetypal, like a primordial scream from something larger. Birth experiences, death experiences, huge reconfigurations of energy. The honest position is not to know what it is, and to trust that there is an intelligence to it. Grounding helps: a barefoot walk in nature, ordinary tasks, sleep.

Practice: tracking fear in the body

Whenever fear, dread or a sense of losing control arises, in sitting or in life. Also once a week as a shadow exercise, step 5.

  2. Find the fear in the body. There is always a change: a contraction in the chest or belly, heat, a shift in the breath, tingling in the hands. If you "don't feel things in the body", look for the subtlest change in the breath; it is always there. This is the second skandha, raw sensation, before the label "fear".
  3. Stay with the sensation and track its whole pattern as it moves. Let it be exactly as strong as it is. You are not making it go away; you are making it conscious.
  4. When there is a little space, inquire: to whom does this fear belong? Who is aware of this body going through this? Rest as that for as long as you can.
  5. Shadow, weekly: bring to mind a person or quality you find repellent. Ask honestly: where is that in me, and what have I exiled to keep it out? Feel whatever the question stirs. Do not fix anything; just let the exile be seen.

Beyond Concepts

The last barriers on the path are the ideas we hold most sacred, including the ideas on this website. Self, no-self, emptiness, awakening: each can become a possession of the ego, and the ego that owns "no-self" is not less of an ego. This stage is about putting the map down.

In plain words

Whether you believe there is an eternal soul or believe there is no self, the belief is the obstacle. Language is dualistic and the truth is not, so every verbal answer misleads. The Buddha, asked point-blank whether there is a self, said nothing. The still point cannot be reached by moving toward it. It is found by ceasing.

The Buddha's silence

The deepest divide in the spiritual world runs between systems that speak of a soul, an Atman, and systems that speak of emptiness and no-self, anatta. Half the great masters use one vocabulary and half the other, and their followers have argued for two and a half millennia. The argument is a symptom of language: we have no non-dual grammar, so every statement lands on one side of a line the truth does not respect.

The Buddha understood this perfectly. Many people are surprised to learn that he never said "there is no self", and that he criticised teachers who taught bare no-self as a doctrine, saying that those who teach only non-self do not become free. When a wandering ascetic named Vacchagotta asked him directly whether there is a self, he stayed silent. Asked whether there is no self, he stayed silent. Afterwards he explained: a "yes" would have confirmed the eternalists in a concept; a "no" would have plunged the questioner into the confusion of thinking he had once had a self and now did not. Both answers lead back into the prison of the conceptual mind.

In his later teachings, the Buddha spoke of an innermost essence that knows no change and no death, calling it the true self, the Buddha-element (buddha-dhatu), or the tathagatagarbha, the embryonic Buddha-nature latent in every being: a hidden treasury. The Mahaparinirvana Sutra says of it that it is real, eternal, stable, peace, untouched by death or harm, indestructible and radiant as a diamond. To a philosopher this sounds like monism. It is not that either. It points past monism, dualism and every "-ism", past perception and non-perception, to what the mind's frameworks cannot hold. The Buddha also said that the awakened one neither exists nor does not exist after death, and meant it literally: the categories do not apply.

We have forgotten the lesson of the silence. We turned soul into a topic for theology class, and "self" and "no-self" and "God" into concepts to be argued for, instead of letting them be revealed through practice, in samadhi and prajna, direct wisdom.

Three marks, not three theories

The confusion is worst around anatta, because it gets used as a philosophical position when it is the report of an experience. Buddhism speaks of three marks of existence, realised through meditation, not debate. Anicca: everything within the field of form is arising and passing away, seen directly. Anatta: nothing within that changing field can be found to be a lasting, independent self. Dukkha: the self-structure is made of craving and aversion, and is therefore inherently unsatisfying. These are experienced by a stripping away of the levels of mind, and they change you. Held as opinions, they change nothing.

It helps to hear anatta as "no identity" rather than "no self". As practice gets subtler, the last perceptual filters (inside and outside, doing and happening, past and future, stuff and awareness) grind down until there is nothing left for identity to attach to. Not because you have decided there is no self, but because the machinery of identification has stopped. "In the seen, only the seen; in the heard, only the heard." You cannot find a sufferer, because identity cannot latch onto anything. That is the end of suffering, and it is a discovery, not a belief. Nor is what remains "nothing": consciousness is not a thing, and it is not nothing. Through meditation, consciousness and the field of change unite, and what is revealed is beyond thing and no-thing.

The spiritual persona

Here is the trap in its final form. Someone has a real glimpse, a genuine samadhi. The mind comes back online. The ego drafts language around the experience, tells the story, takes ownership, and within weeks it has appropriated the insight. Now the person "knows" they are awareness, "knows" there is no self, and says so, and the saying has become a memory added to the self-structure. The moment you express in words who or what you are and identify with what you said, you are back in the dualistic mind. Meditation goes robotic. Inquiry stops, because the ego thinks it already has the answer.

No-self is especially prone to becoming a sacred cow, because it sounds so final. It can be worn as a spiritual persona while the ego remains entirely in charge, certain it is right, disconnected from the heart. This is spiritual bypassing: hiding in the transcendent to avoid the shadow work of Stage 8, to avoid feeling. The tell is simple. If "there is no self" is being used to not feel something, it is bypassing. Releasing samskaras requires a complete experience of the sensations, feelings and emotions that arise, moment by moment, equanimous and inwardly surrendered, and that is exactly what the persona is designed to skip.

The antidote is brutal honesty: watching how desperately we avoid unpleasant feeling, how stubbornly we cling to familiar patterns, how we defend the ego, present ourselves in a good light, distract ourselves from discomfort, and feed the mind entertainment to avoid communion with the present moment. It is possible to be trapped in pattern, stuck in archetypes, playing out karma, while proclaiming there is no one here. The mind knows nothing of its true nature. Keeping that humility is the whole safeguard.

The secret of secrets

The esoteric instruction that runs beneath every tradition is short. The truth of who you are cannot be seen by means of the limited mind. The still point cannot be reached by means of movement. If you want to realise it, let go of all interest in thoughts and sensations, all preferences, all phenomena generated by mind and senses, and rest in naked awareness.

In every moment there are two dimensions: that which changes (thoughts, sensations, the whole field) and that which is aware of change. We are so absorbed in the field that we ignore the awareness. So the investigation begins with becoming aware of awareness, conscious of consciousness. The difficulty is that the limited mind finds this profoundly uncomfortable and will manufacture every hindrance to escape it. The two knots of Stage 3, comfort and knowing, are what pull.

Then the paradox. The mind hears "drop all doing" and wants to know how. But the mind cannot do it, because the mind is movement, an endless process of doing. It will fail in every attempt to realise stillness. The you that you think you are is a process, a collection of patterns, and that pattern has to end for samadhi to be realised. This is the shift from doing to being, and no technique can make it, only the exhaustion of technique.

"Be still, and know." What is being asked to be still? Not the body: it sits on a planet spinning at a thousand miles an hour, orbiting at sixty-seven thousand, the heart beating, the cells dividing. Not the mind: the universe itself, the Hermetic axiom says, is mind, and mind is movement. The stillness meant is something our modern languages have no word for. Sanskrit has shunyata, emptiness, voidness, the ground that does not change and is, more precisely, beyond change and changelessness. When it is realised, stillness and movement are seen to be a duality the mind created. Silence and noise, another. The movement of the world is identical to the stillness. Be still and know; be in motion and know. It is all emptiness dancing.

Rather than something philosophical, this is an entirely different way of interfacing with the world. Actually it is dropping the interface: dropping the reducing valve, which is the self-structure, and meeting your true nature unmediated by the limited mind.

"If awakening is stepping aside and watching the show, is the mind left driving? Is everything scripted? Is there any real choice?" The question is honest and it has a structural answer: it only arises inside duality. It asks whether a separate "I" has free will, and the separate "I" is the illusion. The whole game of question and answer is played on the level of the egoic mind, and as long as we are caught in it we are not awake. Remove the separate "I" and the magic show is revealed: this character is being dreamed within the one mind, and the whole apparatus of choosing versus not choosing falls away with the dream character who was doing the choosing. Samsara and nirvana, the Buddhists say, are one and the same. The world does not change. What drops is the "I" that resisted it, and with it the sense that the show needs a director.

Every "how" question the mind asks about this (how do I be compassionate, how do I stop doing, how do I know what is evil) is the knowing knot asking for a facsimile it can operate. Give it an answer and it will build a persona out of it. The only useful reply is the Buddha's to Vacchagotta.

Go deeperGrinding the filters

An advanced practice, once concentration is stable: hold two poles of a perceptual duality in mind at the same time and let them grind against each other until they collapse. Inside and outside. Doing and happening ("am I doing this or is it happening on its own?"). Self and other. Stillness and movement. Held together with real concentration, the filters wear thin, and when one breaks it stays broken, not only in meditation but all the time. This is how the last layers of identification go: not by belief, but by watching the mechanism that made the distinction until it can no longer make it. Then the great reality is simply as it is: neither self nor other than self.

The Hsin Hsin Ming ends here too: "when thought objects vanish, the thinking subject vanishes", "consider movement stationary and the stationary in motion, and both movement and rest disappear", and then, having gone that far, "although all dualities come from the One, do not be attached even to this One". Even the One, held as a concept, is a concept.

Buddhism: Mahamudra, "the great seal", in the Tibetan Kagyu lineage, is the teaching of resting in naked awareness without fabrication; Dzogchen is its sibling. The Heart Sutra's "no eye, no ear, no nose... no attainment and no non-attainment" is the same clearing of concepts done in verse. Nagarjuna's middle way refuses every position, including the position of having no position.

Hinduism: neti neti, not this, not this, is Vedanta's method of subtraction. The Kena Upanishad: "It is not understood by those who understand it; it is understood by those who do not understand it." The Tao's "those who know do not speak" has a Vedic cousin here.

Taoism: the opening line of the Tao Te Ching is the Buddha's silence in Chinese: the Tao that can be spoken is not the eternal Tao. A mind free of thought, merged within itself, beholds the essence of the Tao.

Christianity: the apophatic or negative theology of Dionysius, Eckhart and the Cloud of Unknowing approaches God by unsaying every attribute. Eckhart prayed to God to rid him of God, meaning the concept. "Be still, and know that I am God" is the practical instruction; its stillness is not of the body or the mind.

Sufism: Ibn Arabi's "unity of being" is also spoken of as beyond both unity and multiplicity. The Sufis distinguish knowledge from books (ilm) from tasting (dhawq); only the latter is knowledge of God, and it leaves the taster unable to say what was tasted.

Practice: resting in naked awareness

Fifteen minutes, after a period of settling. This is the practice the whole path has been preparing for, and it is the simplest one here.

  1. Sit. Let go of every technique. There is no object now, no breath to watch, no label to apply.
  2. Notice the two dimensions of this moment: what is changing (sounds, sensations, thoughts) and what is aware of the changing. Let interest drift from the first to the second.
  3. Be aware of being aware. Do not look for awareness as a thing; that is the mind trying to know. Simply notice that knowing is happening, and rest there.
  4. When the mind produces a concept ("this is emptiness", "I am awareness", "I'm doing it right"), recognise it as one more changing thing, and let it pass without being believed. Even the true ones.
  5. When a hindrance pulls (discomfort, a need to know what is happening), notice which knot it is, and neither obey nor fight. Stay.
  6. Do nothing for the remaining time. Not passivity, which is another doing. Just no interference. Let stillness and movement be whatever they are.

The mind will report that nothing is happening. That report is one more thing appearing in what you are. There is no state to reach, which is why this cannot fail; it can only be interrupted, and interruptions are fine.

Self-check

Why did the Buddha refuse to answer whether there is a self?
Because "yes" and "no" are both dualistic concepts, and either would have led the questioner back into the conceptual mind. Language cannot hold what he was pointing at. Beliefs on either side are obstacles.
What is the difference between anatta as insight and no-self as belief?
The insight is a direct experience, arising when the machinery of identification stops and nothing can be found for identity to latch onto. The belief is an opinion held by an ego, often as a spiritual persona, and frequently used to avoid feeling (bypassing).
What is asked to be still in "be still and know"?
Neither the body nor the mind, both of which are movement. What is meant is the ground that is beyond stillness and movement, for which we have no modern word. When it is realised, stillness and movement are seen as one duality of the mind.
Why can no technique produce the shift from doing to being?
Because the mind is movement, an endless process of doing, and cannot do stillness. Techniques exhaust the mind's attempts; the shift happens when the pattern of "you" gives way, not when it succeeds.
Keep
  • Self and no-self are both concepts. The Buddha's answer was silence.
  • The three marks are experiences, not theories. Anatta means no identity can be found, not "there is nobody".
  • The ego will appropriate any insight into a persona. Bypassing is using the transcendent to avoid feeling.
  • The still point cannot be reached by movement. Rest in naked awareness; the mind's failure is the way.
  • Stillness and movement are one. It is all emptiness dancing.

Stage 10 of 10

Living It

Awakening is the beginning of the path, not its end. What follows is integration: the long, unglamorous, sometimes uncomfortable work of letting the whole human vessel catch up with what has been seen, until every moment is met fresh.

In plain words

The flip can happen in a moment. Stabilising it takes countless small deaths, daily vigilance, and a willingness to feel everything life brings up, which will be more than before. The goal is a mirror mind: reflecting each moment without the distortion of memory. The old has passed; everything is new.

Countless small deaths

The pattern after any real glimpse is predictable enough to be a law. The mind comes back online. The ego drafts a story around the experience, takes ownership of it, and weaves it into a narrative: my awakening. Then the state fades and the story remains, a memory added to the self-structure, and the person starts talking about awakening as if they know. To stabilise and integrate samadhi requires, in the words of the tradition, countless awakenings, countless humblings of the mind, countless little deaths. Each time the mind claims the insight, it has to be seen and released again. This is not failure. It is the fan slowing down.

What sustains it is steady practice, sadhana, day in and day out: sitting, inquiry, watching the mind, freeing the inner energy so that the rewiring can proceed and the inner lotus can grow. Not practice as a technique to be performed, but as continuous presence, the recognition of being, carried into whatever is happening. If you are walking, walk. If you are eating, eat. In the seeing, only seeing. It does not require a cushion, though the cushion helps.

Feeling life

Here is what nobody says at the start: the more work you do on the cushion, the more feelings will come up in life. Not fewer. As the reducing valve opens, what it was filtering out arrives, and the practice must be applied there, in the kitchen and the meeting and the argument, or it is only half a practice.

The instruction is simple to state. Life triggers a stored knot, a samskara, and the body tightens: chest, solar plexus, throat. The habit is to close, to defend, to rationalise, to suppress, to reach for distraction. Instead: relax the body, soften the heart, and let the energy pass through. Do not close. Have a complete experience of the sensation, feeling and emotion as it moves, equanimous and inwardly surrendered. Every thorn you allow to be felt fully is one you no longer have to build your life around avoiding.

This takes brutal honesty, because the ego is extraordinarily skilled at hiding. Watch how it defends its worldview, presents itself in a good light, needs to be right, avoids the unpleasant, clings to the familiar, and feeds itself entertainment to avoid the present. It is entirely possible to be stuck in patterns and archetypes, playing out karma, while quoting non-dual teachings. The tell is always the same: is something being felt, or avoided?

Back in the world

Integration meets its real test in relationships and work, and a few things are worth knowing in advance.

Family and friends know the character. When you stop behaving as the character they know, there is friction. This does not have to be a split; it can be this and that. Trust the inner direction, make the pattern conscious, and stop trying to manage everyone's dynamics; the mind cannot do it and will shut down trying. "No thoughts, no problems. I don't mind what happens."

Other people's pain is contagious. When someone else's pain, what Eckhart Tolle calls the pain body, lands on you and your own picks it up, the mind loves to replay the story. Instead, feel how it moves in the body first, keep attention there, and do not feed the thoughts. The wave is big at first and then smaller each time. Practitioners who have learned not to believe their own thoughts also become good at not believing other people's.

Work and money. The anxious loop of "what should I do for work now?" is a thought pattern like any other. Two questions cut through it: what do you actually want to experience in this game, and what are you willing to pay for it? Where attention goes, energy flows, and a clear answer ("I want to help stressed people slow down; I would do it all day") is usually already known and being blocked by "but how would I make money?". The transitions can take years, and the mind will try to repurpose old patterns in new costumes; catch it when it does.

The energy makes corrections. After a real awakening there is, in practice, no going back into the old shape. Practitioners who have tried report that the freed energy will not allow it: illness, collapse of relationships that only worked for the character, an inner correction of some kind. This is not punishment; it is the current asserting itself. It is also different for everyone.

New abilities. Intuition, creativity, sometimes healing or perception that was not there before: the yogic texts call these siddhis, and warn about them. Do not push them away; do not build an identity on them. A tree needs branches to be healthy, and it still needs to grow toward the sun. When the branches become the whole tree, the path is forgotten.

And expect a period, often uncomfortable, when everything the character says sounds false to you. That is fine. It is part of letting go of what needs to be let go of.

The mirror mind

A mirror reflects whatever is in front of it without holding onto it and without preferring one image to another. The mind that meets each moment this way is called, in Zen, beginner's mind; in the inquiry traditions, the don't-know mind; here, the mirror mind. It is the very essence of meditation and self-inquiry, and it is the destination of this path: not a state, but a way of meeting reality without the mediation of past conditioning.

When you are not experiencing through the filter of memory, each moment is new. Every religion has a word for this. The Christian one is exact: "If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. Old things have passed away; behold, all things have become new." And: unless you become as little children, you will not enter the kingdom. Not childish, but the child's spontaneity, the ease of being that has not yet been strategised. Rumi said silence is the language of God and everything else is poor translation, and yet there is a way for these human vessels to speak from silence, freely, without a little mediator managing the effect. That is authenticity: not a better performance, but life coming through unedited.

Compassion arrives here as a byproduct, not an achievement. Having found out who you are, you have found out who everyone is. A moral code is what a society builds when that direct knowing has been lost; it is downstream of the thing itself. Repair the split at its root and compassion is simply how energy moves through an undivided vessel.

Freedom from, freedom to

Everything up to now has been freedom from: from the character, from suffering, from the fan. There is also freedom to. The awakened person is still here, in the dream, and the dream has other possibilities in it: to evolve, to grow this particular vessel in its unique way, to explore what the traditions call the higher worlds and the other dimensions of ourselves. The energy in the spine, the kundalini, is our evolutionary potential; the centres it moves through, the chakras, are the developing parts of the instrument. At the start the human journey is about survival. Then it expands: relationships, family, creating things in the world, an ever-larger sphere of compassion, until the sense of "I" includes everyone and everything. It is all you.

The highest samadhi, the traditions say, is when this awakened consciousness and the time-bound part of us become one: the vessel matured until awareness shines through it without interruption, a transparent expression of the whole. Always being Buddha; always becoming Buddha. The show goes on. The witness is at peace. The mask is off, and the play is better for it.

To find the truth that you know not, you must go by a path that you know not.

St John of the Cross
Go deeperTuriya: awareness through sleep

The Mandukya Upanishad describes three ordinary states, waking, dreaming and deep sleep, and a fourth, turiya, which is not a state alongside them but the awareness underlying all three. In periods of intensive practice, practitioners sometimes discover that awareness remains when the mind drops off at night: the conditioned self is gone, and something is still present. This cannot be made to happen; it is a byproduct of continuous inquiry. Ramana Maharshi described it as a place where there is no more coming and going, an opening into the timeless, for as long as it lasts. Its stabilised, open-eyed form is sahaja, the natural state, in which primordial awareness is recognised as ever-present while life and death arise within it.

Turiya is the experiential answer to the question of Stage 9: what does not change? Not the waking self, which dissolves each night. Not the dreamer. Not the blank of deep sleep. What knows all three.

Practice: relax, release, and the evening mirror

Part one: in life, whenever something closes you. Part two: five minutes before sleep, every night.

  1. Notice the closing. Someone says the thing, the email arrives, the memory surfaces. Feel the tightening before you feel the story: chest, belly, throat, jaw.
  2. Relax the body. Drop the shoulders, soften the belly, loosen the jaw. Do not wait until you feel better; do it while it hurts.
  3. Keep the heart open. Do not defend, rationalise or suppress. Let the energy of the moment move through the chest instead of being held there. Feel it completely. It passes faster when felt than when managed.
  4. Speak or act from the other side of it, if speaking or acting is needed. Notice how different that is from speaking from inside the contraction.
  5. Evening mirror. Before sleep, replay the day once, briefly. Where did the character defend itself, need to be right, avoid something, present a good face, reach for distraction? Name each honestly, without punishment. Then let the whole day go. Rest as what watched it. Tomorrow is new.

If the same closing keeps returning, it is a groove, and it will keep returning until it has been fully felt. That is not a failure of the practice. It is the practice.

Self-check

Why does life get more intense, not calmer, after real practice?
Because the reducing valve is opening. What it filtered out now arrives to be felt. The practice has to be applied in life, not only on the cushion; more feeling is the sign it is working.
What does "countless little deaths" refer to?
The repeated releasing of the ego's claim on each insight. The mind appropriates the glimpse into a story; that story must be seen and let go, again and again, until the pattern runs down.
What is the mirror mind?
Meeting each moment without the mediation of past conditioning: beginner's mind, don't-know mind. Reflecting what is, without holding or preferring. To live this way is to be awake; in Christian terms, to be a new creation.
What is "freedom to"?
Beyond freedom from the character lies the freedom to evolve: to grow the vessel, expand the sphere of "I" until it includes everything, and let awareness shine through a matured human instrument. Always becoming Buddha.

Field guide to what comes up

The path is not abstract. The same things arise for almost everyone who sits and inquires seriously, and most of them are frightening only when they are unexpected. This guide collects the questions that come up in retreat after retreat, with the answers the teaching gives.

First, triage

When turbulence arises in practice, it helps to know which of four things it is, because they call for different responses.

In sitting

I keep falling asleep in meditation. How do I overcome it?
Notice the question first: the "I" that wants to overcome sleepiness is part of the sleepiness. Two approaches, both valid. Inhabit it: bring the whole pattern into view (heaviness around the eyes, the feeling in the head, the change in the breath, the slump or sway in the posture), how it changes in intensity, whether it is continuous or in waves. Fully seen, it loses its power. Or inquire: to whom does this tiredness belong? The mind-body is tired; who is aware of the tired mind-body? That can produce an instant flip. Also check the obvious: lying down almost always means sleep, and sitting is what lets energy build.
My legs are restless, my skin itches, I feel like screaming if I don't move. What is this?
Unless there is a medical reason, it is almost certainly samskaras surfacing: resistance held in the body. Ask who wants to move. Is it the conditioned mind seeking relief and comfort, or energy beginning to move the body on its own? Dissipating the build-up through movement or qigong brings relief but not the breakthrough; there is an alchemy that only happens when the little self reaches the point where something has to give. Find the middle way: past the comfort zone, not into overwhelm. The body scan is the specific tool for this.
My body moves on its own: shaking, swaying, spontaneous postures, sounds. Is something wrong?
These are kriyas, described in the kundalini traditions: freed energy moving the body into new configurations, with an intelligence of its own, as samskaras diminish and energy is rerouted into new pathways. Not everyone gets them; some get subtle movement, some dramatic. Do not conceptualise them and do not perform them. They are a perfect moment to inquire: is there a sense that I am doing this? Who is moving? When inquiry is present they usually integrate quickly. If they persist for a long time, the mind has probably become involved ("this is awesome, I'm doing something"). Yes, kriyas can help clear samskaras even when you have no conscious idea what is being cleared; trust the intelligence and hold fewer concepts about it.
My mind runs scenarios continuously and I'm gone before I notice.
Use the labelling technique. Every thought is visual, auditory, or both. When a thought snags you, note its type, do not enter the story, and return to the anchor. Even heavy thoughts (about money, lawsuits, someone trying to take everything) pop like a balloon when noted. Recurring patterns become visible and drain. You are not trying to kill the monkey mind; you are making it fully conscious.
I have chronic back and neck pain and can't sit long. Can I go on retreat?
Yes, and most retreats have only a few hours of mandatory sitting. Use props, a wall, a chair. This is biological pain, so take care of it. Practically, a year or two of ordinary stretching yoga or qigong has made the difference for many people whose hips or spines would not let energy rise. And within the sitting, sometimes surrender lets the energy break through the discomfort and create new pathways; explore that edge without brutalising yourself.
I get dry throat, ringing, and my vision starts blacking out. Keep inquiring?
No. Those are the body's warning signs, especially if you have fainted before. Take a time out. Fainting is unproductive; you could hit your head. It might be a samskara, but there is no way to verify that, and the body is asking for attention. Use discernment; there is a middle way, and this is on the "listen to the body" side of it.
My environment is noisy. Should I use white noise?
A monk in Las Vegas once said anyone can be a monk on a mountain; the city is where the practice gets hard, like running with weights on. If the noise is truly overwhelming the nervous system, use sound. Otherwise explore dropping the concept ("annoying leaf blower") and feeling the raw vibration as it arrives. Middle way: do not overwhelm yourself, do not make it too easy either.
I settle myself with controlled breathing before I sit. Is that fine?
Some settling breathwork is fine. Then notice whether the controller has stayed in charge. Try letting the breath be completely natural, letting the body be breathed. What happens when the one managing it relaxes?
Does it matter what meditation object I use?
Not much. Breath, the third eye, sensations, energy, a chant: each is a bridge into the same field of prana. If the breath feels like it has no location for you, use the brow point. If concentration is good and energy is flowing, techniques are doing their job; eventually they fall away into simply sitting.

Energy, visions and strange states

I see geometric patterns, kaleidoscopes, colours, diamonds, and get "information".
Freed energy produces phenomena in any of the senses: visuals, sounds, sensations, changes of consciousness. It sounds like one of the absorption states, a sign of moving in the right direction. Treat it as phenomena: do not get too interested, do not shut it down, keep practising. Sometimes symbols are messages from another level of mind, for integration, rather than "just" phenomena. Either way, do not build an identity on them.
My body and mind vanished. Was I asleep?
The test is awareness. If you were zoning out into dreamland, use concentration techniques. If awareness was present while body and mind dropped away, and you came back calm and refreshed, that is a deep stage of absorption, not sleep. Keep exploring; it will clarify itself.
A huge energy release: full-body shaking, a primordial scream, hours of twitching afterwards, no sleep. It felt like it wasn't mine.
In deep clearing, what surfaces sometimes feels generational or collective, and the honest position is not to know exactly what it is. There is an intelligence to it; trust the process. Ground afterwards: barefoot walking in nature, ordinary tasks. If it comes again in sitting and you can lie down without sleeping, lying can let the energy do its work more easily. Otherwise, observe, surrender, stay equanimous, and let it do its thing.
Forty-five minutes of struggle, then bliss, then I didn't want to come back. Is the up and down normal?
Completely. And notice the preference: you want to stay in the good state. The Zen poem's whole teaching is no preferences. States, even beautiful ones, come and go. Who is the knower of those states? The awareness has no preference; let the states come and go and you will experience them more, not less. Sometimes the murky session that excavates the depths is the most beneficial one.
I feel no emotions any more. I can't label things good or bad.
Read that again. Not being able to label things good or bad is not a malfunction; it may be the disease of the mind going quiet. Keep exploring gently. If awareness and aliveness are present, this is likely a stage. If there is flatness and disconnection from the heart, look for bypassing.
I don't feel anything in my body. It's all thoughts.
That is important information, and it points to where to work. Every thought, especially a fearful one, produces some change in the body: a contraction, a shift in the breath. Track the subtlest change. The body scan is the tool to develop this; if you get lost in thought halfway through, that is the practice, not a failure. When attention stays at the sensory level instead of feeding the mind, the thought loses its power and becomes an energetic experience.

Glimpses and losing them

I had a glimpse years ago. Things are subtly, permanently different, but it also feels lost. There's a gap.
The mind recognises only the state that was there, and the state is temporary. The awareness that was present in the glimpse is the awareness here now. The mind is comparing today's phenomena to a memory of phenomena; what you are is not an experience and cannot be remembered, only recognised. Let the memory go and keep doing the inquiry; it is only a clearing process, and one of these times you intend to directly experience and there you are.
I had the union experience, lost it, and became a desperate seeker.
This is the most common story on the path, and it has a shape: union arrives to someone who still believes they are the character, the character claims it, it fades, the seeker is born. Years of knowledge follow and move you further away. The seeker eventually fails completely; the mind is humbled and admits it knows nothing; and in that failure the flip becomes possible. Stage 6 covers it. You are not doing it wrong; you are in the middle of it.
Is the real Self a constant joy, or an empty space emotions pass through?
Within mind and body there is a continuum from union-bliss to hellish separation, and every state is on it. What you are is the awareness of the whole continuum. When identity flips to that, there is a peace not contingent on anything external: not the joy at the nice end of the line, but the relief of coming home to the truth. What knows this? That has to be experienced, not answered.
Is the Self already awake?
Yes. Awareness has been watching the whole drama the whole time; it has no quality, so it is not asleep or awake. What flips is the sense of "I": from entangled with the character to recognised as awareness. So inquire into that "I". Is it the character, or something closer?
I shared something in a group and afterwards my ego took ownership: "I did that well."
Everyone recognises this. When the little self takes ownership, and it will, do not identify with it; smile at it like a child, love it in spite of its delusion. Practitioners who have done this report something behind the acceptance, a golden nugget: appreciating the greatness present too, without either grasping or rejecting. Even the seeker, accepted completely as a small contracted thing, opens into "I am also that", and then the seeker has nothing to seek.
There's a blanket of false silence and I know something's underneath.
Look gently. Practitioners describe seeing the blanket, looking softly, and the first things to surface being "I'm not safe" and "I'm scared". Those are roots, and the dyad is often where they come out because they need the space of being witnessed. What comes up in inquiry is not answers to your life situations; it is the beliefs that keep you trapped in them.

Fear and darkness

Sheer terror and panic: I'm going to die, I'm going crazy, I don't know what's real.
This is the primordial fear of Stage 8 and it is a good sign: you are touching the root of the "I"-thought, and nobody gets an easy ride there. The fear has no object; it is fear itself, the ego fearing oblivion. It is the exact moment to inquire: who does this fear belong to? Who is watching this character let go? Reassure the character: it is moving to the back seat, not dying. Track the fear as sensation; do not feed the narrative. With repetition the identical energy becomes bliss. And if equanimity is completely gone, ground and return later. Psychological stability matters for this work; if life is destabilised, get support alongside practice.
I came close to letting go, then scenarios flooded in: paralysed, homeless, a burden to my family. I snapped back.
The mind generates every worry about what happens if control is released. Nothing is lost except the sense of "I" attached to the character. Life continues as it is; nothing external needs to change. The pattern of "come close, get scared, go back to seeking robotically" is worth naming to yourself, because naming it is most of the work. Then feel the fear at the level of raw sensation, make it fully conscious in the body, and the knot can release.
A dark, destructive "tower" energy rises in me. It feels needed, like a guardian. Samskara or part of me?
Reframe the question: is there any resistance to any part of yourself? The angel and the demon are one energy; the fallen angel is the angel turned ego. Push away the dark and you push away the light with it, and the exile returns through the world as a mirror. Integration does not mean acting it out or condoning darkness, and it does not mean befriending everyone who carries it. It means noticing what is being resisted, feeling it fully, seeing it as it is, and letting it back into the one. Made conscious, it loses its power over you.
I did a thought experiment where nobody recognised me, and it broke something.
The mind, deprived of external identity, immediately wrote a story (homeless, picking up trash). That is the knowing knot refusing not to know. The inquiry is not "what am I if nobody acknowledges me" but "who is aware of the one needing acknowledgement?" The insight that arrived later, "it's not me", is the right direction. Stay with that.

Inquiry and dyads

In dyads I only report mundane sensations, then doubt I'm doing it right.
Weather reports. Report the doubt too, and then feel the frustration and pointlessness and let that take you deeper. Look for what is just under the surface, held back, wanting to be expressed. Go toward feeling, not analysis, almost like a child playing.
"Who am I?" makes me tense. I have to find out before I die and I can't be happy until I do.
The striving is the mind in motion, wanting something other than what is, and it does not even know what it wants. Drop the striving; it is not working, and it is an enormous burden. If being present is easy and only the question is tense, then be present, and inquire into who is present. It is not a question; it is an imperative to convey who you are with your whole being. The mind will keep looking for a verbal answer. Let it fail. What you are seeking is right here when the seeking relaxes; it is a cosmic joke.
My partner started prompting me: "no, tell me who you ARE, tell me about awareness."
That breaks the technique. The witness must be totally neutral: no commentary, no prompting, no feedback. Prompting is for trained facilitators who have awakened and are clear. If what is arising for you is a dark or "unspiritual" energy, reporting it is exactly right; clearing what blocks the direct experience comes first.
Can my spouse witness and facilitate me?
Witness, yes. Facilitate, no: keep the roles strict and the witness silent. Dyads between partners work and can be beautiful; they also stir decades of material. Treat what comes up as clearing, not as the next conversation.
In dyads there's joy, laughter, presence. Then on the cushion the mind is all over the place trying to recreate it.
The mind will never get it, and that is the only thing the mind has to learn: that it has no place in this process. Be careful, because even that sentence gives it something to do. Being is always here; the mind does not have to look for it. Just abide, and let the mind generate what it generates. On the cushion it is the same practice as the dyad: recognise being, and whatever needs clearing will come. If nothing comes up to be cleared, perhaps it is coming up on the cushion instead. It is all one practice.
How do I do self-inquiry alone?
Settle. Receive the imperative from yourself, or simply become aware of being. Intend to directly experience what you are. Notice whatever arises as a result; let it be fully felt and cleared; return to the intention. Repeat. It is the dyad without the mirror.
Techniques throw me into full identification; only relaxing and allowing works.
Then that is the right approach for now. Whatever allows the mind to settle and lengthens the periods of just being. Sitting through overwhelming identification with no equanimity at all is unproductive. The insight that "doing" was the problem is itself important. Even equanimity comes and goes; be equanimous with that.

Life

My family knows me as the character, and I'm in no man's land between them and the path.
When you stop behaving as the character people know, there is friction. It does not have to be a split: this and that, not this or that. Make the pattern conscious, trust the inner direction, and notice the focus on outcome. Trying to control the whole family dynamic is beyond the mind and produces overwhelm. Be honest, then let go. No thoughts, no problems; I don't mind what happens.
What should I do for work now that I'm on the path? I've been paralysed for months.
Two questions: what do you actually want to experience in this game, and what are you willing to pay for it? When someone answers "I'd help stressed people slow down all day, I'd never stop", the next question is why they are not doing it, and the answer is always "but how would I make money?", which is a thought. Transitions take time; the mind will try to repurpose the old pattern in a new costume; catch it. Where attention goes, energy flows.
Should I see a therapist about my need for approval, or just keep practising?
Two approaches, always: work within the character (developmental lines, therapy, trauma work) or wake up from identification with the character entirely. Both are legitimate; the second cuts at the root in one stroke, if you simply stop believing the character's thoughts. In practice, life tests this over and over. If the psyche is unstable, do both.
Psychic abilities, spirit guides, healing: obstacle or gift?
The siddhis. Everything in the field of changing phenomena is ultimately maya and not necessary for liberation, but these things arise for some people and should be neither pushed away nor built into an identity. A tree needs branches; it also needs to grow toward the sun. Watch for the moment "healer" becomes who you are and the path gets forgotten.
Someone else's pain keeps landing in my heart and the mind replays the story.
The pain body picks up other pain bodies and loves the material. Feel it in the body first, keep attention there, do not feed the thoughts. The wave is big, then smaller. Not believing your own thoughts makes you good at not believing other people's too. Pray if that is your way; feeling it and letting it move is the practice.
Should I abstain from sex before a retreat?
Who is asking? The character asks for permission; both indulging and repressing keep you on the level of mind. The third option is to spend the time inquiring into who you are, continuously, and let go of anything that does not lead to that. When energy is going into cravings, it is not free for the exploration; when it becomes free, the "I am" appears vividly. Different patterns are strong for different people; identify yours.
How do I tell the current of life from my small mind? In hindsight my "following the current" was a mistake.
Going against the current has a feel: struggle, resistance, like a boat turning into the river. Preference within the ego is that resistance. But energy explores; it goes here and there, and the path is not always linear or clear. Mistakes happen to everyone on it, including the ones who teach. Trust life, and hold your own certainty lightly; the farmer's "maybe" applies to your own decisions too.
Can there be an authentic conversation, when the deepest thing has no words?
Silence is the language of God and everything else is mistranslation, Rumi said. And yet a small child, before the personality is built, speaks with complete spontaneity. When the filter drops, energy moves through freely, and what comes out conveys who you are by its aliveness rather than its content. If your spouse asks "who just said that?", that is a good question to sit with.
The teacher still has an ego, doesn't he?
Ask anyone who lives with one. Every character is a work in progress. If it were otherwise you should drop everything and only meditate; since it is not, keep practising and keep living.

Glossary of terms

The original words, in Pali, Sanskrit, Chinese, Arabic, Greek and Hebrew, carry precision that English translations lose. Each entry gives the term, its source language, and the meaning it has on this path.

The self and its machinery

skandha Sanskrit · Pali: khandha
"Aggregate" or "heap". The five processes that together manufacture the sense of a self: form, sensation, perception, habit patterns, consciousness. Stage 2.
rupa Sanskrit / Pali
Form. The first skandha: the body, the physical basis on which contact occurs.
vedana Sanskrit / Pali
Sensation. The second skandha: raw sensory input before labelling, neither good nor bad. The layer where samskaras are felt and released.
sanna Pali · Sanskrit: samjna
Perception. The third skandha: recognition and naming from memory ("mosquito").
sankhara Pali · Sanskrit: samskara
Habit patterns, volitional formations. The fourth skandha: the layer of craving and aversion, where preference and the sense of "me" are thickest. Also, as samskara, the individual impressions stored from past experience: the grooves that karma follows. Stage 3.
vijnana Sanskrit · Pali: vinnana
Consciousness. The fifth skandha: the faculty of attention that observes the other four, like a camera. Still part of the mechanism; empty of self.
the I-thought English · Sanskrit: aham-vritti
Ramana Maharshi's term for the root thought "I" from which all other thoughts arise. Defined on this path as the resistance to what is. Self-inquiry traces it to its source.
self-structure English
The whole conditioned apparatus: the five skandhas, the samskaras, the persona. The character. Also called the reducing valve, because it filters reality down to what the ego can manage.
maya Sanskrit
The play of appearances; illusion in the sense of a magic show. The entire field of changing phenomena, including the inner witness.
nafs Arabic
The self or ego in Sufism, purified through stages from the "commanding" nafs (driven by craving) to the nafs "at peace".
the shadow English (Jung)
The disowned, unconscious parts of the psyche, which run one's life until made conscious, and which return through the world as mirrors. Stage 8.
pain body English (Tolle)
Accumulated emotional pain that feeds on drama and picks up the pain of others. A modern name for a cluster of samskaras.

Momentum

karma Sanskrit
Literally "action". On this path: the tendency of energy to follow established pathways. Inertia, not bookkeeping.
vasana Sanskrit
Latent tendency; the seed-form of a habit that has not yet fired.
klesha Sanskrit
Affliction. Patanjali's five: ignorance, egoism, attraction, aversion, clinging to life. The engines of the whirlpool.
chitta vritti nirodha Sanskrit
"The cessation of the whirlpool of the mind." Patanjali's definition of yoga in the second sutra.
the two knots English
The body's attachment to comfort and the mind's attachment to knowing: the two primary bonds of ego identification.
conditions of no escape English
A retreat structure (or daily discipline) that removes the ego's exits, so that what it avoids has to surface.

Practice

sadhana Sanskrit
Spiritual practice. Before awakening: techniques and effort. After: abiding as awareness. Ramana Maharshi: "I have never done any sadhana."
dharana Sanskrit
Concentration; single-pointed attention on an object. Sixth limb of Patanjali's yoga; the effortful half of practice.
dhyana Sanskrit · Pali: jhana · Chinese: Chan · Japanese: Zen
Meditative absorption. The seventh limb. Root meaning: "to burn up", i.e. to burn up the samskaras. Stage 6.
samadhi Sanskrit
Union; the collapse of subject and object. The eighth limb. With seed (savikalpa, sabija): temporary, a self-structure seed survives. Seedless (nirvikalpa, nirbija): the mind-body drops off entirely.
samyama Sanskrit
The last three limbs, dharana, dhyana and samadhi, practised as one. Effort and surrender merging.
vipassana Pali
Insight. The Buddhist practice of observing phenomena with equanimity, developed as the systematic body scan.
atma-vichara Sanskrit
Self-inquiry. Turning attention toward the source of the "I". Ramana Maharshi's method. Stage 7.
the dyad English
Paired inquiry: a neutral witness gives the imperative "tell me who you are"; the inquirer intends to directly experience their nature and communicates whatever arises. Developed in the 1960s for intensive retreats.
the imperative English
"Tell me who you are." Not a question but a command to convey oneself with one's whole being.
koan Japanese
A Zen question that cannot be answered by the intellect ("what was your original face before your parents were born?"), used to exhaust the thinking mind.
dhikr Arabic
Remembrance; the Sufi practice of repeating the Name until the rememberer disappears.
prana / chi Sanskrit / Chinese
Life energy. "Chi follows consciousness": energy flows where attention goes. The pranic field is what every meditation object bridges into.
piti Pali
Rapture; the release of energy characteristic of the first jhana, felt as tingling, waves, light or joy.
sukha / dukkha Pali
Satisfactoriness / unsatisfactoriness. The shift from dukkha to sukha marks entry into absorption; the preference for sukha is the next thing released.
kriya Sanskrit
Spontaneous movement, posture, sound or breath that arises when freed energy moves the body. Also the name of a yoga lineage of energy techniques.
kundalini Sanskrit
The evolutionary energy coiled at the base of the spine; its rising through the chakras is the maturing of the human instrument.
chakra Sanskrit
"Wheel." The energy centres of the subtle body; the developing parts of the vessel. The move from brow to crown is where primordial fear often arises.
mudra Sanskrit
A seal or gesture, sometimes arising spontaneously in absorption.
siddhi Sanskrit
Power or ability (intuition, healing, perception) that may arise on the path. Not necessary for liberation; a potential identity trap.
hindrance English · Pali: nivarana
Classically five: sense desire, ill will, sloth, restlessness, doubt. On this path: not obstacles but the unconscious material arriving to be made conscious.
productive / unproductive pain English
Karmic pain (samskaras surfacing; sit through it) versus biological pain or overwhelm (the body's intelligence; take care of it).

Realisation

awakening English · Japanese: kensho, satori
The immediate recognition of unconditioned being; the flip of identity from character to awareness. Always available; the beginning of the path.
liberation English · Sanskrit: moksha
The stabilisation of awakening; the maturing of the vessel until awareness shines through without interruption.
the flip English
The figure-ground reversal in which the sense of "I" moves from the character to awareness.
anicca Pali
Impermanence. First of the three marks of existence, realised experientially: everything in form arises and passes.
anatta Pali · Sanskrit: anatman
Non-self. Second mark: nothing in the field of change can be found to be a lasting self. Better heard as "no identity": the machinery of identification stops. A direct insight, not a doctrine.
dukkha Pali
Unsatisfactoriness. Third mark: the self-structure is made of craving and aversion and is therefore inherently unfulfilling.
Atman / Brahman Sanskrit
The Self / the absolute ground. Vedanta's teaching is that they are not two. Held as belief, an obstacle; realised, the truth.
tathagatagarbha Sanskrit
"Womb (or embryo) of the Thus-Gone." Buddha-nature latent in every being; the hidden treasury. Also buddha-dhatu, the Buddha-element.
shunyata Sanskrit
Emptiness, voidness. Not nothingness: the ground that is beyond both change and changelessness. "It is all emptiness dancing."
sat-chit-ananda Sanskrit
Being, consciousness, bliss. The uncaused bliss of awareness recognised, not produced by any skandha.
turiya Sanskrit
"The fourth." The awareness underlying waking, dreaming and deep sleep, which may be discovered to persist through sleep in intensive practice. Mandukya Upanishad.
sahaja Sanskrit
The natural state: samadhi stabilised with open eyes, in ordinary life.
jivanmukta Sanskrit
One liberated while living.
nirvana Sanskrit · Pali: nibbana
"Blowing out." The cessation of the unconscious patterns that constitute the limited "I", and of the suffering that attends them. Samsara and nirvana are one and the same; only the "I" drops.
prajna Sanskrit
Direct wisdom, as opposed to conceptual knowledge.
Mahamudra Sanskrit
"The great seal." Tibetan teaching of resting in naked awareness without fabrication. The source of the "secret of secrets" instruction in Stage 9.
fana / baqa Arabic
Annihilation of the self in God / subsistence in what remains. The Sufi map of awakening and integration.
wu wei Chinese
Non-action, non-forcing. Acting without the extra layer of grasping; moving with the Tao like water.
Tao Chinese
The Way; the current of life. "The Tao that can be spoken is not the eternal Tao."
beginner's mind English · Japanese: shoshin
The don't-know mind, the mirror mind: meeting each moment without the mediation of past conditioning. The essence of practice and the mark of being awake.
spiritual persona English
The ego rebuilt around a spiritual insight or concept ("I am awareness", "there is no self"), often more stubborn than the original ego.
spiritual bypassing English
Using transcendent concepts to avoid feeling and shadow work.
I AM English · Hebrew: Ehyeh asher ehyeh
The name given to Moses; Jesus's "before Abraham was, I am". The traditions' pointer to pure awareness prior to any predicate.
new creation English
"If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation; old things have passed away." The Christian name for living without the filter of memory.

Mythic figures

Arjuna / Krishna Bhagavad Gita
The struggling human ego facing an impossible battle / the awakened Self, who says: let me go in your place.
naga Sanskrit
Serpent. The seven nagas around the Buddha or Patanjali in ancient iconography: the dimensions of the unconscious, integrated and at attention.
eros / thumos Greek (Plato)
The black horse of desire and the material / the white horse of spirited aspiration toward the higher. Both are harnessed; neither is killed.
Mara Pali / Sanskrit
The lord of illusion who assailed the Buddha on the night of awakening with fear and desire. He touched the earth and remained.
Lucifer Latin
"Light-bearer." The most beautiful angel who split from the One: the ego, "rather to rule in hell than serve in heaven". The demon and the angel are one energy.

A note on the map

Two cautions. First, these are correspondences, not equations. The yoga sutras and the Buddhist jhana maps agree about ninety percent of the time, which is astonishing for independent systems, but the ten percent is real and the traditions argue about it. Second, the map is itself a concept, and Stage 9 applies to it. A person can memorise every column and be no closer than when they started. The table's only use is that when something arises in your own practice, you can recognise it in five languages and be less afraid.

The deepest agreement is the simplest. Every one of these traditions holds that what is being sought is already present, that the seeker is what obscures it, and that the way is a subtraction rather than an acquisition. The Tao Te Ching says it in a line: in the pursuit of the Tao, every day something is dropped.

The teaching travels in pictures because the mechanism it describes cannot be seen directly by the mind that runs it. Here is every image used on this site, with what it points at and where it is developed.

The riverStage 1

Life is a current flowing back to its source; awakening is a point in that flow, not a summit to climb. Resisting the current is suffering; swimming with it is choiceless awareness.

The TV dramaStage 1

You are the one on the sofa, not the protagonist on screen. Absorption in the drama is not bondage; remembering where you sit is not liberation. It is the end of a mistaken identity.

The martial artistStage 1

The mosquitoStage 2

Fixation on shadows (thoughts, images, perceptions) mistaken for reality. Awakening is walking out into the sun, and there is no real going back.

The child and the squirrelStage 2

How the false self begins: a child's attention goes naturally to aliveness; the teacher says pay attention; the child learns that following its energy gets it into trouble, and builds a persona to please.

The cameraStages 2, 4

Concentration turns and focuses, narrow or wide; the fifth skandha is this camera. Awareness is not the camera; it is what the camera and its pictures appear in.

The handStage 2

Put attention on your hand and it tingles. Nothing changed but where attention went. Chi follows consciousness; the self-structure is powered by being looked at.

The riverbedStage 3

Samskaras are channels cut by past water; karma is water following them. Practice is the outside force that stops feeding the channel until it fills in.

The ceiling fanStage 3

Awakening pulls the plug; the blades keep spinning on momentum. Every reaction to a preference plugs the fan back in. Practice is noticing the hand on the socket.

The two knotsStage 3

The body tied to comfort, the mind tied to knowing. Every hindrance is one of the two pulling. Present without choosing loosens both.

A thorn to remove a thornStage 4

Techniques are used to interrupt the pattern of the mind and then discarded. Kept, they become a new layer of the self-structure.

Companion: making the thief the chief of police, Ramana's warning about letting the mind run the practice.

Oil from a jarStage 4

Patanjali's image of attention: a continuous stream, not drops. Concentration chooses the object; attention keeps it.

The eye of the birdStage 4

Arjuna sees only the eye of the bird. Single-pointedness: not the tree, the crowd, the arrow, or the clever answer that there is no bird.

Mount CarmelStage 4

St John of the Cross: climb straight, look neither left nor right, do not stop for the flowers, do not be scared off by the creatures.

The bridgeStage 4

Breath is the bridge; energy is the bridge. Any meditation object leads into the same pranic field, where watcher and watched merge.

The candle burning at both endsStage 4

Inhabit the phenomena, or inquire directly: the self-structure is one candle, and it does not matter which end burns.

Weights onStage 4

A noisy apartment or a hard life is training with weights on, said the monk in Las Vegas. Weights can be too heavy; only you can feel the line.

WaterStage 5

Benefits everything, competes with nothing, seeks the low places, and overcomes the rigid by yielding. Wu wei is not passivity; water is never in conflict with the shape of things.

The wheel and the cupStage 5

Thirty spokes meet at the hub but the hole makes the wheel turn; clay makes the cup but the emptiness holds the water. Usefulness lives in what is not there.

The farmer's horseStage 5

Runs away (bad luck?), returns with wild horses (good?), the son breaks his leg (bad?), the army passes him over (good?). Maybe. I don't know. The mind is not qualified to judge life.

Eating the menuStage 6

Maps of the jhanas, and this website, describe the meal. Do not mistake reading the menu for eating.

Companion: mistaking the map for the territory; dharma as checkboxes.

The continuumStage 6

A line from union-bliss at one end to hellish separation at the other; every moment is somewhere on it. What you are is the awareness of the whole line, and is at neither end.

The beggar on the chest of goldStage 6

Begging for coins while sitting on a treasure, never looking inside. Seeking pleasure in form while the bliss of pure being is under you.

Dry kindling, wet woodStage 4

Some ignite at a direct pointer; others need time to dry. Most are wet wood for a while, and then are not.

Companion: the Buddha's flower sermon, where one student in the assembly understood.

The only game you win by giving upStage 7

Self-inquiry. Give up control, give up knowing, surrender, and in the stillness of surrender experience the truth.

The gapStage 7

The felt distance between a remembered glimpse and now. There is no gap; there is a memory of phenomena being compared to present phenomena, while awareness is unchanged in both.

The roller coasterStage 8

Hold on and the energy is fear; let go and the same energy is aliveness. Two ways to ride: as the empty witness (peaceful, removed) or non-dually (emptiness and fullness together, life itself riding).

Cold waterStage 8

Plunge in tense and it is shock; let go and, in time, the nervous system learns the shock was the resistance. The same is true of the letting-go the little self fears.

Driver's seat, back seatStage 8

What feels like death to the character is the conditioned self moving from driver to passenger. It stays on the team; the ride gets more interesting.

Companion: the conditioned mind makes a good servant but a poor master.

The Wizard of Oz; the snake and the ropeStage 8

Fear is a terrifying facade with nothing behind the curtain. Seen clearly, the snake is a rope, and there is nothing to fear and never was.

Dante's gateStage 8

"Abandon all hope, you who enter here." Hell is the ego structure; to pass through it, abandon hope and fear together, because they are one mechanism.

Luke's caveStage 8

Told he will not need his weapon, he takes it, strikes down Darth Vader, and the mask opens on his own face. The thing feared most in the dark is a disowned part of oneself.

Companion: Joseph Campbell, "where we had thought to slay another, we shall slay ourselves".

The nagasStage 8

Seven serpents fanning behind the awakened one in the old temples: the dimensions of the unconscious, integrated and at attention. Not slain. The hero rides the dragon.

The fallen angelStage 8

The most beautiful angel splits from the One: "rather to rule in hell than serve in heaven". That is the ego, my will not Thine. The demon and the angel are one energy.

The chariot and two horsesStage 8

Black horse eros pulls toward the material; white horse thumos toward the spiritual. Kill neither; drive both. Lose one and you lose half your power or leave the road.

The world as mirrorStages 8, 10

What is pushed away in oneself returns through relationships, triggers and synchronicities. The person who repels you is showing you an exile.

The reducing valveStage 9

The self-structure filters reality down to what the ego can manage. Awakening is dropping the interface, not improving it.

The spinning planetStage 9

"Be still" cannot mean the body, on a globe turning at a thousand miles an hour, or the mind, which is movement. The stillness meant is beyond stillness and movement: emptiness dancing.

Grinding the filtersStage 9

Hold both poles of a duality (inside/outside, doing/happening) together with real concentration until they wear thin and break. Once broken, they stay broken.

The sacred cowStage 9

"No-self" held as a belief and worn as a persona. Not less of an ego for being made of a true idea.

Countless small deathsStage 10

Each time the ego claims an insight, the claim must be seen and released. Stabilising awakening is this, repeated, until the fan has stopped.

The thorn in the heartStage 10

A stored knot that life keeps brushing. Build a life around avoiding it, or feel it fully once and be free of the avoidance. Relax, release, keep the heart open.

Branches and the sunStage 10

New abilities are branches; a healthy tree needs them. It still needs to grow toward the sun. When the branches become the whole tree, the path is forgotten.

The mirrorStage 10

Reflects whatever is in front of it without holding or preferring. The mirror mind meets each moment without the mediation of memory. Old things have passed away.

The inner lotusStages 1, 10

The maturing of the human vessel after awakening: freed energy grows the instrument until awareness shines through it permanently. Always becoming Buddha.

Reference

The blueprint

The whole teaching as one system: a baseline, three inputs, a crucible, and four outcomes. This is the view to build a course or an app from, because it shows what each practice does to what, and in which order things tend to happen.

1 · Baseline

The false self

  • The five skandhas manufacture "me" moment by moment
  • Samskaras store unfelt charge in body and mind
  • The I-thought: resistance to what is
  • Two knots: comfort (body), knowing (mind)

Stages 1–3

2 · Inputs

The practices

  • Meditation: concentration + equanimity; labelling; body scan
  • No preferences: the farmer's mind; questioning thoughts
  • Self-inquiry: to whom does this belong? who am I?
  • The dyad: witnessed inquiry; peer-to-peer clearing

Stages 4, 5, 7

3 · Crucible

Processing, and its errors

  • Samskaras surface as hindrances and karmic pain
  • Energy frees: piti, kriyas, absorption states
  • Primordial fear at the root of the I-thought
  • Errors: chasing states; conceptual traps; the spiritual persona; bypassing; overwhelm

Stages 6, 8, 9; field guide

4 · Outcomes

The system state

  • Absorption: layers of mind burn; consciousness changes
  • Awakening, the flip: identity moves from character to awareness
  • Liberation: the inner lotus grows; the vessel rewires
  • Non-dual abiding: the mirror mind; a new creation

Stages 6, 9, 10

The loop

The system runs as a loop, not a line. A practice (zone 2) interrupts the baseline (zone 1). The interruption pushes material into the crucible (zone 3): a samskara surfaces, karmic pain is met, fear is triggered, and, if equanimity holds and the story is not fed, the energy is rerouted from resistance into aliveness. Each pass through the loop produces a little more of zone 4: a state, a glimpse, a stabilisation. Each pass also offers an error: the state is grasped, the glimpse is appropriated into a persona, the fear tips into overwhelm. The error routes back to zone 1 as a new samskara. The correction is always the same: no preference, feel it fully, ask who it belongs to.
